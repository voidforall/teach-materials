The Limit Order Book

The order book is the arena your bot lives in. Learn its rules — price–time priority, depth, mid, microprice, queue position — because every quoting decision is a bet on where you sit in it.

An exchange matches orders with a strict algorithm. The limit order book is the data structure: all resting limit orders, grouped by price, sorted by time within each price. Two order types matter to you:

  • Limit order — "buy at most P / sell at least P". It rests in the book until matched or cancelled. Makers use these.
  • Market (or marketable) order — "execute now at any price". It crosses the spread and consumes liquidity. Takers use these. Many venues charge taker fees and pay (smaller) maker fees or rebates — check your venue's maker/taker schedule, because it changes your effective spread.

Matching rules: price–time priority

Best price wins first; at equal price, earliest order wins. This has one huge consequence for market makers: queue position is an asset. An order first in line at the best bid gets filled before everyone else at that price — and, crucially, gets filled exactly when fills are dangerous (fast markets eat the top of the book first). Orders deep in the queue are filled less often, and more often when the price has traded through you — i.e., adverse selection is baked into queue mechanics.

The prices you'll quote around

  • Best bid (b) / best ask (a) — the tightest resting orders on each side.
  • Mid = (a + b)/2 — the naive fair value. Free to compute, but ignores which side is bigger.
  • Microprice = (b·Qa + a·Qb)/(Qb + Qa) — a size-weighted mid that leans toward the side with more resting size. If a huge bid wall sits at 99.9, the microprice pulls below the mid: the true clearing price is slightly lower. Most production bots quote around microprice, not mid.
  • Depth — total size at each level. Book pressure (bid depth ≫ ask depth) is a short-horizon predictor.
Keystone Reference price + offset = quote. Every quoting algorithm in this course has the shape "pick a fair value (mid/microprice/reservation price), then post at ± a computed offset." Lesson 3 makes the offsets economic; Lesson 4 makes them optimal.
Anti-pattern Quoting on the mid when the book is one-sided. Mid says 100.00, but 5,000 lots sit on the bid and 100 on the ask. The next move is almost certainly down; quoting symmetric around 100.00 means your bid is stale the moment it's posted. Skew your reference first — that's what the microprice is for.

Retrieve it

Two orders at the same price — which fills first?

The earlier one.

Price–time priority. Arriving early at a price level is worth money — it raises your fill probability at that price without changing the price.

Bid 99, ask 101, bid size 10,000, ask size 10. Where does the microprice sit?

Just below 100.

It weights each side by the opposite side's size, so a heavy bid pulls the reference down. You'd quote both sides lower than a symmetric-around-mid bot.

Why is being at the front of the queue a double-edged sword?

More fills, worse fills.

Front position maximizes fill probability — including in fast markets where the price is about to trade through your level. Queue mechanics concentrate adverse selection at the front.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 15 minutes Write 30 lines of Python: subscribe to (or poll) any public order book, compute mid, microprice, and imbalance (bid size − ask size)/(bid size + ask size), and print them each update for five minutes. Watch whether microprice leads mid in the direction of the next move. This code becomes the reference-price module of your bot.
