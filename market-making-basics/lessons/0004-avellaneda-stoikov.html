Avellaneda–Stoikov Quoting

The most-cited formula in retail market making. You don't need its stochastic-control derivation — you need the two equations, what each parameter does, and how they degrade in a real bot.

Avellaneda & Stoikov (2008) pose the market maker's problem exactly as Lesson 3 framed it: earn spread, penalize inventory quadratically, over a finite horizon T. Their solution is two formulas your bot can compute every tick.

Formula 1 — the reservation price

r(s, t, q) = s − q·γ·σ²·(T − t)

Instead of quoting around the market price s, quote around a reservation price r that shifts against your inventory. Long (q > 0)? Your reference drops, so both quotes sit lower — you make it cheaper for the market to buy from you (reducing q) and more expensive to sell to you. The shift is:

  • proportional to q — bigger inventory, bigger inducement to shed it;
  • proportional to σ² — volatile market, more urgent to be flat;
  • proportional to (T − t) — early in the session, plenty of time left, skew gently; near the end, skew hard to finish flat.

Formula 2 — the total spread

δa + δb = γ·σ²·(T − t) + (2/γ)·ln(1 + γ/k)

Half of this on each side around r gives your bid and ask. The second term involves k, the fill-intensity decay: how quickly fill probability falls as you quote further out (from the intensities λ(δ) = A·e−kδ they assume). Read it as:

  • volatility term — compensation for inventory risk while your quotes are resting;
  • k term — compensation for lost fill probability: the market only pays for width if trades still happen.

Pseudocode for your bot

each tick:
  s   = microprice                    # Lesson 2
  vol = EWMA of trade returns         # sigma
  q   = current inventory
  tau = max(T - t, eps)               # time remaining
  r   = s - q * gamma * vol**2 * tau  # reservation price
  width = gamma * vol**2 * tau + (2/gamma)*log(1 + gamma/k)
  quote_bid = r - width/2
  quote_ask = r + width/2
Keystone A–S = "skew your reference by inventory, widen by volatility." Even if you never implement the exact solution, this is the shape of every serious quoting engine: reference price shifted by −qγσ²τ, half-spread growing with σ. The paper's value is proving this shape is optimal under clean assumptions, not that the constants are sacred.
Anti-pattern Treating γ and k as true parameters. They're tuning knobs. γ is risk aversion (how much you hate inventory); k is a fitted decay of fill probability. Calibrate k by regressing observed fill rates on quote distance; set γ to the largest inventory skew you're comfortable with, then halve it.

Retrieve it

You're long 5 lots and σ doubles. What happens to r?

It drops, a lot.

The shift is −qγσ²τ: doubling σ quadruples its effect while inventory multiplies it. Both quotes move down hard — you're now actively bribing the market to take your inventory.

Why does the optimal spread grow as (T − t) shrinks near the end?

It doesn't — the inventory term shrinks.

Careful: the τ in the spread multiplies the remaining inventory risk, which vanishes at T. What grows near T is the skew relative to time available: less time left to work inventory off, so the reservation price shifts harder per unit of q.

What real-world input do you need that the paper assumes away?

Fill intensity vs distance.

A–S assume λ(δ)=A·e−kδ with known k. Your bot must estimate fill probability from its own (or the market's) fill history — and update it when the regime changes.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 30 minutes Implement the pseudocode above in your simulator with a random-walk mid (σ from real data if you have it). Run three configurations: γ=0 (symmetric quoting — A–S degenerates to quoting around s), moderate γ, huge γ. Plot inventory paths and P&L. You should see: γ=0 accumulates drunken inventory; moderate γ trades spread for flatness; huge γ quotes so wide it rarely trades.
