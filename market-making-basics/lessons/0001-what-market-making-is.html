What Market Making Is

A market maker buys from impatient sellers and sells to impatient buyers, thousands of times a day, and tries to keep the spread. It's a business of earning small edges repeatedly while dodging people who know more than you.

Most trading strategies are directional: predict the price will go up or down, take a position, wait. Market making is the opposite bet. You express no opinion on direction. You simultaneously post a bid (an order to buy) below the market and an ask (an order to sell) above it:

  • Someone sells into your bid — you acquire inventory.
  • Someone buys from your ask — you release inventory.
  • If both happen near the mid price, you pocket the bid–ask spread minus fees.

Do that a million times a year and the spread adds up. The catch: you are trading against whoever hits your quote, and some of them are trading because they know something. That's adverse selection, and it's the reason naive market makers bleed out.

The market maker's identity

Harris (Trading and Exchanges, 2003) describes a market maker as a trader whose profits come from liquidity supply, not prediction. Exchanges and regulators often formalize the role (designated market makers with quoting obligations), but anyone resting limit orders that others trade against is doing market making. Your bot will be exactly that: a liquidity-supplying algorithm.

The three enemies of every market maker

  1. Adverse selection — informed flow picks off stale quotes. Your fill is often bad news: the price was moving away from you.
  2. Inventory risk — you accumulate a position, and the price moves against it.
  3. Costs — exchange fees, and in some venues, the spread you must cross to hedge.

Everything in this course — quoting algorithms, skews, risk limits — exists to manage these three. Keep them in mind as a checklist for every design decision in your bot.

Keystone Market making = sell liquidity, don't predict. Your gross profit per round trip is the spread; your losses come from being filled when you'd rather not have been. Every algorithm in this course is a rule for when and where to post quotes so that the fills you get are the profitable kind.
Anti-pattern Market making as a disguise for direction. If your bot quietly accumulates inventory because "the price will come back", you're no longer earning the spread — you're holding a directional bet with extra steps. The whole discipline (Lessons 5 and 7) is about refusing that temptation.

Retrieve it

Where does a market maker's gross profit come from?

The bid–ask spread.

Buy at the bid, sell at the ask, repeat. Net profit subtracts fees, adverse-selection losses, and inventory mark-to-market moves.

Name the market maker's three enemies.

Adverse selection, inventory risk, costs.

Quoting algorithms earn their spread against enemy #1; inventory skew (Lesson 5) handles #2; fee-aware quoting and hedging handle #3.

You get filled on your bid. Why might that be bad news?

Fills are negatively selected.

Traders most eager to sell are often those who know the price is heading lower. A fill is information: the market just told you it wanted your bid. Lesson 6 turns this into measurable toxicity.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 10 minutes Open any live order book (e.g. BTC/USDT on Binance or Nasdaq AAPL). Watch the best bid and best ask for two minutes. Note how often the spread widens just before big moves — that's other market makers defending against enemy #1 in real time.
