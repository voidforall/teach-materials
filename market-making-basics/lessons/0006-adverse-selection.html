Adverse Selection & Flow Toxicity

Your fills are a sample of the market's most motivated traders. Learn to measure when that sample is toxic — and the four defensive reactions your bot needs before it can survive real flow.

Lesson 3 defined adverse selection as the post-fill drift against you. Here's how to measure it live and what to do about it. The unifying idea, from Glosten–Milgrom and the microstructure literature (Hasbrouck's Empirical Market Microstructure is the reference): every trade carries information, and your quotes are filters that decide how much of it you absorb.

Measuring toxicity

  • Post-fill drift (per fill). Your own experience: average mid move in the N seconds after your fills, by side. Directly comparable to your half-spread.
  • Order-flow imbalance. Over a rolling window, signed volume (buys +, sells −). Persistent one-sided flow is someone working a large position — the classic toxic regime.
  • VPIN (volume-synchronized probability of informed trading, Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara 2012): buckets trades by volume, estimates the fraction of bulk buckets where flow is one-sided. High VPIN historically preceded toxicity events; controversial but cheap to compute and a decent regime flag.
  • Spread-inside-spread. When the inside spread widens sharply but quoted depth is thin, other market makers have already fled — respect their opinion.

The four defensive reactions

  1. Widen — increase half-spread multiplicatively with a toxicity signal. Buys margin on each fill.
  2. Fade — move quotes in the direction of the flow (again the microprice idea): if flow is hammering bids, drop your reference now, don't wait to get filled.
  3. Reduce size — keep the prices, cut the quantity. Limits per-fill damage while keeping queue presence.
  4. Pull — cancel the endangered side entirely. The last resort and the most important one: around scheduled news (CPI, earnings, Fed) production bots pull both sides, because the next print can gap straight through any spread you'd reasonably quote.
Keystone Toxicity is a regime, not a constant. The same quotes that print money in calm two-sided flow lose money the moment one informed participant starts working an order. Your bot doesn't need to predict toxicity — it needs to detect it from its own fills and flow, and have pre-wired reactions (widen / fade / shrink / pull) it can execute instantly.
Anti-pattern Quoting through scheduled news. The market maker's dream fill — someone crosses a huge spread to trade with you — is usually someone who knows the number before you do. There is no spread wide enough around a CPI print for a small bot; the only correct quote is no quote. Keep an economic calendar and flat-out stop quoting in the minutes around releases.

Retrieve it

Your post-fill drift stat just tripled. What does that mean, precisely?

Fills got more expensive.

Each fill now costs, on average, three times the mid-drift it used to. If that exceeds your half-spread your per-fill edge is negative — widen, shrink, or pull before volume amplifies the bleed.

Name the four defensive reactions in order of escalation.

Widen, fade, reduce, pull.

Each is costlier in lost spread income but cheaper in tail damage. Your bot should map toxicity-signal levels to this ladder, mechanically.

Why is quoting around news a no-go even with a huge spread?

Prices gap, not glide.

Post-news the first trades can print far through any quoted spread; your resting order fills at the old price and the mark immediately moves against you. The distribution has no width you can charge for.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 30 minutes In your simulator, add an "informed trader": occasionally inject a sequence of one-sided market orders that move the mid (a worked order). Log your bot's fills during these episodes vs. calm periods and compute the post-fill drift statistic separately. Then wire one defense — e.g. widen ×2 when rolling flow imbalance exceeds a threshold — and watch the drift-loss line shrink.

Primary source

Read this Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara, "Flow Toxicity and Liquidity in a High-frequency World" (2012) — the VPIN paper; read it for the measurement idea. Hasbrouck's book has the rigorous foundations.
