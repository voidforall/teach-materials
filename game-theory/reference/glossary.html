Glossary

The course's shared vocabulary. Lessons adhere to these definitions. Grows as we go.

Ordered roughly from foundations outward, not alphabetically — read top-to-bottom the first time, jump back by Ctrl/Cmd-F after.

The game object

Game

A model of interaction where each participant's outcome depends on everyone's choices, not just their own. Minimum spec: players, actions, payoffs.

Player

A decision-maker. Assumed rational (picks actions to maximize their own payoff) and, unless stated, that this rationality is common knowledge (everyone knows that everyone knows… that everyone is rational).

Action vs. Strategy

An action is a single move. A strategy is a complete plan: what you'd do at every point you might have to move. In one-shot simultaneous games the two coincide; in sequential games they differ sharply.

Payoff (utility)

A number ranking how much a player likes an outcome. Higher = better. Only the ordering matters for most solving; the units are not money unless said so.

Normal (strategic) form

A game written as a payoff matrix: rows = one player's actions, columns = the other's, each cell = the payoff pair (row's, column's). Convention: row player's payoff is listed first.

Extensive form

A game written as a tree of sequential moves — who moves when, what they know, and payoffs at the leaves. Used for sequential games (see backward induction).

Solving: the core toolkit

Best response

Given a fixed choice by the others, your best response is the action(s) giving you the highest payoff. The atomic operation of game theory — solving a game is mostly "everyone best-responding to everyone."

Dominant / dominated strategy

A strictly dominant strategy is best no matter what others do. A strictly dominated strategy is worse than some other option no matter what others do — a rational player never plays it. Weak versions replace "strictly better/worse" with "at least as good/never better."

Iterated elimination of dominated strategies (IEDS)

Delete dominated strategies repeatedly; each deletion can expose new dominated strategies in a smaller game. What survives is the set of rationalizable plays.

Nash equilibrium (NE)

A profile of strategies where every player is best-responding to the others simultaneously — no one can gain by unilaterally deviating. A "no regrets, given what everyone else did" resting point. May be pure or mixed; a game can have zero, one, or many.

Pure vs. Mixed strategy

Pure: commit to one action. Mixed: randomize over actions with set probabilities. Nash's theorem: every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium once mixed strategies are allowed.

Indifference principle

The method for finding a mixed equilibrium: each player sets their own mixing probabilities so that the opponent is indifferent between the actions they mix over. Consequence — your equilibrium probabilities are pinned down by the other player's payoffs, not your own. To solve a 2×2: write the opponent's expected payoff to each action as a function of your mix, set them equal, solve.

Backward induction

Solve a sequential game from the last move backward: at each decision, assume future players will act optimally, and fold that back. Yields the subgame-perfect outcome.

Subgame-perfect equilibrium (SPE)

A Nash equilibrium that is also a Nash equilibrium in every subgame — rules out equilibria propped up by non-credible threats. The refinement that makes sequential games behave.

Credible vs. non-credible threat/promise

A threat or promise is credible only if carrying it out is in the actor's own interest when the moment comes. Backward induction / SPE discards equilibria that rely on non-credible ones (e.g. "I'll fight any entrant" when fighting would only hurt you).

Commitment

A move that binds your own future choices before a rival acts, changing what they expect you to do. Counterintuitively, removing your own options can raise your payoff (excess capacity, binding contracts, escrow, reputation, burning a bridge). The strategic core of bargaining and deterrence.

Hold-up problem

An efficient deal fails because one side can't credibly promise to follow through after the other sinks a cost (e.g. a supplier who'd renege once paid). Fixed by a commitment device: enforceable contracts, escrow, or a repeated relationship.

Recurring structures & market games

Prisoner's Dilemma

Each player has a dominant "defect," yet mutual defection is worse for both than mutual cooperation. The canonical clash between individual and collective rationality.

Repeated game & discount factor (δ)

A stage game played over and over by the same players. The discount factor δ ∈ (0,1) weights each future round — read as patience or as the probability the relationship continues. A known finite horizon unravels cooperation by backward induction; an infinite/uncertain horizon can sustain it.

Grim trigger / Tit-for-tat

Enforcement strategies in a repeated game. Grim trigger: cooperate until the first defection, then defect forever — sustains cooperation when δ is high enough (e.g. δ ≥ ½ for the standard PD). Tit-for-tat: cooperate first, then copy the rival's last move — nice, retaliating, forgiving, clear; won Axelrod's tournaments.

Folk theorem

For patient players (δ near 1), almost any payoff profile above each player's punishment (minmax) level can be sustained as a subgame-perfect equilibrium of the infinitely repeated game. Explains contract-free cooperation — at the cost of predicting a large multiplicity of equilibria.

Zero-sum game

One player's gain is exactly the other's loss (payoffs sum to a constant). Pure competition; solved by minimax.

Reaction (best-response) function

Best response generalized to continuous actions: a function giving your optimal move for each possible move by the rival. With linear demand, Cournot reaction functions slope down ("you make more ⟹ I make less"). The Nash equilibrium is where both players' reaction functions intersect.

Cournot / Bertrand competition

Two models of duopoly. Cournot: firms choose quantities; equilibrium output per firm is (a−c)/3 under demand P=a−Q, landing between monopoly and perfect competition. Bertrand: firms choose prices, and undercutting drives price to marginal cost.

Bertrand paradox

With identical goods and price competition, just two firms suffice to push price to marginal cost and profit to zero — the competitive outcome. Resolved in practice by capacity limits, product differentiation, or repetition. Lesson: the strategic variable (price vs. quantity) can matter more than the number of firms.

Private vs. common value

Private value: the item is worth a different, self-known amount to each bidder. Common value: worth the same unknown amount to all, and each has only a noisy estimate (breeds the winner's curse).

First- vs. Second-price auction

First-price: highest bidder pays their own bid — you must shade below your value, and there's no dominant strategy (optimal bid depends on beliefs). Second-price / Vickrey: highest bidder pays the second-highest bid, making truthful bidding a weakly dominant strategy — your bid sets only whether you win; others' bids set the price.

Revenue-equivalence theorem

Under standard assumptions (independent private values, symmetric risk-neutral bidders), first- and second-price auctions yield the same expected revenue — bidders' shading in the first-price format cancels its apparent advantage.

Winner's curse

In common-value auctions, winning means you were the most optimistic bidder — so winning is itself bad news about the value. Rational bidders shade down to correct for it.

Asymmetric information

One side knows something payoff-relevant the other doesn't — typically their own type (quality, ability, a private signal). Breaks the common-knowledge-of-payoffs assumption behind earlier lessons; the source of adverse selection and signaling.

Adverse selection

Hidden information before trading drives good types out: any price attracting the good type overpays for the pool, because bad types imitate the same offer. Akerlof's market for lemons. Real-world remedy in markets: the bid–ask spread compensating a market maker for the risk of trading against informed flow.

Signaling & the single-crossing condition

The informed party takes a costly action to reveal type credibly. It only works if the signal is differentially costly — strictly cheaper for the type sending it than for a mimic (else it's cheap talk and reveals nothing). Spence's education signal is the classic.

Separating vs. pooling equilibrium

Separating: different types send different signals, so behavior reveals type. Pooling: all types send the same signal, revealing nothing and leaving beliefs at the prior.

Screening

The mirror of signaling: the uninformed party designs a menu (insurance deductibles, product versions) so that different types self-select — mechanism design applied to hidden types.

Mechanism design

"Game theory in reverse": design the rules so that self-interested play produces the outcome you want (e.g., the Vickrey auction designed so honesty is optimal). The CS/markets bridge.

Price of anarchy

Ratio of the social cost at the worst Nash equilibrium to the optimal cost. Measures how much selfish play degrades a system — a workhorse of algorithmic game theory.
