Repeated Games & Sustaining Cooperation

"We'll meet again" is a contract enforced by the future. When it is, and exactly how patient you must be.

In a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, both players defect — it's dominant, and cooperation is a fantasy. Yet real firms, traders, and neighbors cooperate all the time without a contract. The missing ingredient is repetition: when the same players meet again and again, the shadow of future punishment can make cooperation rational today. This lesson gives you the skill to compute exactly how patient players must be for cooperation to hold.

The stage game

Here's the one-shot game they play each round — a standard Prisoner's Dilemma (higher = better):

Row: Cooperate(3, 3)(0, 5)

Defect dominates, so the one-shot equilibrium is (Defect, Defect) = (1, 1) — worse for both than the (3, 3) they can't reach. Now play it repeatedly.

First a warning: a known last round unravels

Finite horizon → backward induction bites If both players know the game ends after, say, round 100, cooperation collapses. In round 100 there's no future to protect, so both defect (it's dominant). Knowing that, round 99 has no cooperation to preserve either → defect. The logic unravels all the way back: always defect. This is Lesson 3's backward induction, and it means cooperation needs an infinite or uncertain horizon — a game that might always continue.

So model the future as ongoing, with a discount factor δ (0 < δ < 1) on each next round. Read δ two ways, both useful: how much you value future payoffs, or the probability the relationship continues one more round. High δ = patient, or a long expected relationship.

The enforcement strategy: grim trigger

Consider this plan for both players: "Cooperate every round — but the instant the other defects, defect forever after." That's the grim trigger. Is sticking to cooperation a subgame-perfect equilibrium? Compare a player's two options, valued as a stream:

  • Keep cooperating forever: 3 each round → 3 + 3δ + 3δ² + … = 3/(1 − δ).
  • Defect once, then eat the punishment: grab the tempting 5 now, but trigger defection forever after (1 each round) → 5 + δ·1/(1 − δ) = 5 + δ/(1 − δ).

Cooperation holds when cooperating is at least as good as deviating:

3/(1 − δ) ≥ 5 + δ/(1 − δ)  ⟹  (3 − δ) ≥ 5(1 − δ)  ⟹  4δ ≥ 2  ⟹  δ ≥ 1/2.

The result If players value the future at least half as much as the present (or the relationship continues with probability ≥ 50%), mutual cooperation is a subgame-perfect equilibrium of the repeated game. The one-shot's "impossible" (3, 3) becomes stable — enforced not by a contract, but by the credible threat of losing all future cooperation. This is how repetition rescued Lesson 3's supplier promise and can sustain Lesson 4's Bertrand collusion.

A gentler enforcer: tit-for-tat

Grim trigger is unforgiving — one slip and cooperation dies forever. Tit-for-tat — "start by cooperating, then copy whatever the other did last round" — is the famous alternative from Axelrod's tournaments. It's nice (never defects first), retaliating (punishes defection immediately), forgiving (returns to cooperation the moment the rival does), and clear. Those four traits made it dominate real strategy competitions — a rare piece of theory with a crisp practical prescription.1

The big picture: the folk theorem

Folk theorem Grim trigger sustaining (3, 3) is one instance of a sweeping result: if players are patient enough (δ near 1), essentially any outcome that gives each player more than their guaranteed one-shot "punishment" payoff can be supported as a subgame-perfect equilibrium of the repeated game. The upside: it explains how cooperation, cartels, and norms survive without contracts. The catch: it predicts a huge multiplicity of equilibria — theory tells you cooperation is possible, not that it's inevitable. Which one you land on is about focal points, history, and trust.

Check yourself

Q1. Why can't cooperation be sustained in a Prisoner's Dilemma with a known final round?

Backward induction: the last round defects, unraveling cooperation to the start.

Needs an infinite or uncertain horizon so there's always a future to protect.

Q2. What are the two readings of the discount factor δ?

How much you value future payoffs, or the odds the game continues.

Both make "high δ" mean the future weighs heavily enough to deter defection.

Q3. (Spaced recall — Lesson 5) In a second-price auction, what should you bid?

Your true value — it's weakly dominant regardless of others' bids.

Your bid decides only whether you win; the price is the top rival bid.

Now you drive

Try it — find the patience threshold before revealing

Same repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, but the temptation is milder: both-cooperate = 3, tempt (defect on a cooperator) = 4, both-defect = 1, sucker = 0. Using grim trigger, how patient must players be — what's the threshold on δ — to sustain cooperation?

Reveal the solution

δ ≥ 1/3.

Cooperate forever: 3/(1 − δ). Deviate once then punished forever: 4 + δ/(1 − δ). Hold cooperation when 3/(1 − δ) ≥ 4 + δ/(1 − δ) ⟹ (3 − δ) ≥ 4(1 − δ) ⟹ 3δ ≥ 1 ⟹ δ ≥ 1/3. Compare to the worked case (temptation 5 → δ ≥ 1/2): a smaller temptation to cheat lowers the patience you need. The intuition generalizes — the bigger the short-term gain from betrayal, the more the future has to matter to hold the deal together.
