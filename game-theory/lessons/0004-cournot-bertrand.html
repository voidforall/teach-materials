Market Games: Cournot & Bertrand

Real firms don't pick from two boxes — they pick a number. Best response becomes a function, and the equilibrium is where the curves cross.

Every game so far had a small grid of actions. But a firm's move is a quantity or a price — a continuous number. The whole toolkit still works; two things just upgrade: a best response becomes a reaction function, and a Nash equilibrium becomes the point where both reaction functions intersect. This lesson makes that concrete on the two workhorse models of competition — and ends with a jolt: the same two firms can earn healthy profits or zero, depending only on whether they compete on quantity or price.

Cournot: firms choose quantities

Two firms make an identical good and each pick how much to produce, q₁ and q₂. The more total output Q = q₁ + q₂ hits the market, the lower the price it clears at:

Price  P = a − Q  = a − q₁ − q₂ ; each unit costs c to make.

Firm 1's profit is revenue minus cost: π₁ = (P − c)·q₁ = (a − c − q₁ − q₂)·q₁. Now find Firm 1's best response — the q₁ that maximizes π₁ for a given q₂. Take the derivative w.r.t. q₁ and set it to zero (the top of the profit hill):1

a − c − 2q₁ − q₂ = 0  ⟹  q₁ = (a − c − q₂) / 2

Reaction function That last line is Firm 1's reaction (best-response) function: "however much you make, I make half of what's left of the market." The more the rival produces, the less you do — the curves slope down. Firm 2's is the mirror image. The Nash equilibrium is the one pair (q₁, q₂) that lies on both curves at once.

Where the curves cross

Take clean numbers: a = 12, c = 0. Firm 1: q₁ = (12 − q₂)/2. Firm 2: q₂ = (12 − q₁)/2. By symmetry set q₁ = q₂ = q:  q = (12 − q)/2 ⟹ 3q = 12 ⟹ q = 4.

Each firm's best-response curve slopes down; they cross once — the Cournot equilibrium.

Read the outcome: total Q = 8, price P = 12 − 8 = 4, and each firm earns (4 − 0)·4 = 16. Compare the two extremes:

  • Collude like a monopoly (jointly restrict output): they'd make Q = 6, price 6, and split 36 → 18 each — better for them, worse for buyers.
  • Perfect competition: price gets driven to cost 0.

Cournot sits between: rivalry erodes the monopoly profit (16 < 18) but doesn't erase it. Add more firms and the equilibrium slides toward the competitive price — the model's headline result.

Bertrand: firms choose prices — and the paradox

Same two firms, same constant cost c, identical product. But now they post prices, and every buyer goes to the cheaper one. What's your best response to a rival pricing at p?

Undercut them — set your price a hair below p and capture the entire market, as long as you're still above cost c. But your rival reasons identically. So each keeps undercutting the other…

The Bertrand paradox …until price hits marginal cost c, where neither can profitably cut further. The unique equilibrium is p₁ = p₂ = c, with zero profit. Just two price-setting firms reproduce the perfectly-competitive outcome. Same firms as Cournot — profit 16 each there — now make nothing. The strategic variable you compete on (quantity vs. price) can matter more than how many of you there are.

That's genuinely surprising, and its "resolutions" are where real markets live — each one relaxes an assumption:

  • Capacity limits — you can't actually serve the whole market, so you can't fully undercut (this connects back to the commitment idea — capacity is a credible pre-move).
  • Product differentiation — if goods aren't identical, a slightly cheaper rival doesn't steal all your buyers; prices settle above cost.
  • Repetition — firms that meet repeatedly can sustain higher prices by threatening to punish undercutting (next lesson's territory).

Check yourself

Q1. With continuous actions, what is a Nash equilibrium, geometrically?

The point where both players' reaction curves intersect at once.

Same idea as double-underlining: each is best-responding to the other simultaneously.

Q2. Why do two Bertrand firms end up pricing at marginal cost?

Each can grab the whole market by undercutting, until no cut stays profitable.

Any price above cost invites a rival to shave a penny and take everything.

Q3. (Spaced recall — Lesson 3) Why doesn't a "we'll start a price war" threat always deter entry?

If fighting hurts the incumbent too, the threat is non-credible and ignored.

Backward induction keeps only threats the mover would actually carry out.

Now you drive

Try it — derive the equilibrium before revealing

Two firms compete à la Cournot. Demand is P = 8 − Q (with Q = q₁ + q₂) and each unit costs c = 2. Find Firm 1's reaction function and the symmetric equilibrium quantity per firm.

Reveal the solution

Reaction: q₁ = (6 − q₂)/2.   Equilibrium: q₁ = q₂ = 2 each.

Profit π₁ = (8 − q₁ − q₂ − 2)·q₁ = (6 − q₁ − q₂)·q₁. Derivative w.r.t. q₁ set to 0: 6 − 2q₁ − q₂ = 0 ⟹ q₁ = (6 − q₂)/2 — same shape as the worked case with (a − c) = 6. Symmetric: q = (6 − q)/2 ⟹ 3q = 6 ⟹ q = 2. Then Q = 4, price P = 8 − 4 = 4, and each firm earns (4 − 2)·2 = 4. Sanity check: monopoly would set Q = (a−c)/2 = 3 (price 5, profit 9) — more than the duopoly's 4+4 split of 8, exactly as expected: competition shrinks the pie's price and each slice.
