Auctions & Mechanism Design

Design the rules and you design the behavior. Why one auction makes honesty the smart move — and one makes winning bad news.

An auction is a game where the rules are the design choice. Ad exchanges, treasury bonds, spectrum sales, and the matching logic inside an order book are all auctions. This lesson delivers the field's most beautiful practical result — a rule under which telling the truth is a dominant strategy — and then flips the whole subject around into mechanism design: game theory run backward, choosing payoffs so that self-interest produces the outcome you want.

The setup: private values

You want an item worth v to you (your private value — only you know it). You submit a bid b. Your surplus is v − (what you pay) if you win, else 0. The two classic sealed-bid rules differ only in what the winner pays:

  • First-price: highest bidder wins and pays their own bid.
  • Second-price (Vickrey): highest bidder wins but pays the second-highest bid.

The gem: in a second-price auction, bid your true value

Truthful bidding is a dominant strategy In a second-price auction, bidding exactly b = v is weakly dominant — it is optimal no matter what anyone else bids. The reason is a clean split: your bid decides only whether you win; the others' bids decide how much you pay. So you want to win precisely when the price (the top rival bid) is below your value — and bidding your value does exactly that, automatically.

Here's the whole proof, by the same case-checking as Lesson 1's dominance. Let m = the highest bid among your rivals (that's the price if you win). Compare bidding your true value v to any deviation:

  • Bidding above your value (b > v). It only changes the outcome when v < m < b: now you win, but pay m > v → negative surplus. You bought a loss. Truthful bidding would have let you lose, which was correct. Never better.
  • Bidding below your value (b < v). It only changes the outcome when b < m < v: now you lose — but you'd have happily paid m < v for surplus v − m > 0. You threw away a profitable win. Never better.
  • In every other case the deviation changes nothing (you win/lose the same and pay the same m).

So no deviation ever helps and some deviations hurt: bid v. Notice how the design does the work — the rule makes honesty individually rational, so you don't need to model your opponents at all. That is the point of good mechanism design.

First-price: now you must shade

Change the rule to "pay your own bid" and truthful bidding becomes a mistake: bidding v wins you zero surplus. You must shade — bid below your value. But how far? Bid too low and you lose; bid too high and you win with thin surplus. The sweet spot depends on what you believe rivals will bid — so unlike the second-price auction, there's no dominant strategy, only a Nash equilibrium in beliefs.1

Revenue equivalence — the punchline You'd think "pay-your-own-bid" earns the seller more. It doesn't. Under standard assumptions (independent private values, symmetric bidders), first-price and second-price auctions yield the same expected revenue. The bidders' shading in the first-price auction exactly cancels the seller's apparent advantage. This revenue-equivalence theorem is a foundational, non-obvious result — the format is often a choice about robustness and simplicity, not raw revenue.

The trap: the winner's curse

Everything above assumed private values. Flip to a common value — an asset worth the same unknown amount to everyone (an oil tract, a mispriced block of shares), and each bidder only has a noisy estimate. Now something nasty appears:

Winner's curse If everyone bids near their estimate, the winner is whoever most over-estimated the value — so winning is itself bad news: it tells you your estimate was the rosiest in the room. Rational bidders correct for this by shading down, bidding as if their estimate were conditioned on already being the highest. Ignore it and you systematically overpay. (Directly relevant to bidding for assets of uncertain value — a very trading-flavored failure mode.)

Zooming out: mechanism design

Every lesson so far analyzed a given game. Mechanism design asks the inverse question: "I want a certain outcome — what rules make self-interested players produce it?" The Vickrey auction is the emblem: its designer wanted efficient allocation and truthful bids, and got both by choosing the payment rule cleverly. Its generalization (the VCG mechanism) underlies real ad auctions and combinatorial allocation — the CS/markets frontier of the subject.2

Full-circle with Lesson 2 Remember the audit game's lesson — to change someone's behavior, change the payoffs they face, not your own. Mechanism design is that principle industrialized: pick the rules so that the payoffs make honesty, effort, or efficiency the selfish choice. Analysis finds the equilibrium; design chooses the game so the equilibrium is the one you wanted.

Check yourself

Q1. In a second-price auction, why is bidding your true value optimal?

Your bid sets only whether you win; the price is others' top bid.

So you want to win exactly when that price is below your value — bid your value.

Q2. What is the winner's curse, in one line?

Winning a common-value item means you likely over-estimated its worth.

The highest bidder is the most optimistic estimator, so winning signals overpayment.

Q3. (Spaced recall — Lesson 4) Two identical-good firms compete on price. Where does price land?

At marginal cost, with zero profit — the Bertrand paradox.

Each undercuts the other until no profitable cut remains.

Now you drive

Try it — argue it out before revealing

A second-price auction. Your true value is 50. You're tempted to "get a deal" by bidding 40. Show that bidding your true value 50 weakly dominates bidding 40, by checking two scenarios: the highest rival bid is 30, and the highest rival bid is 45.

Reveal the solution

Bidding 50 is never worse and sometimes strictly better — so bid 50.

Rival bid 30: Bid 50 → win, pay 30, surplus 20. Bid 40 → still win, pay 30, surplus 20. Tie.
Rival bid 45: Bid 50 → win, pay 45, surplus 5. Bid 40 → lose, surplus 0. Bidding 40 forfeits a profitable win.
Shading to 40 only ever "protects" you in cases where winning was already unprofitable (rival above 50) — and there, bidding 50 loses too. So it gains nothing and can cost you the 45-case surplus. That's exactly the general proof, made concrete: your bid should equal your value.

Notes & citations

  1. For values drawn independently and uniformly on [0, 1] with n bidders, the symmetric equilibrium first-price bid is b = (n−1)/n · v — you shade less as the field grows, because competition forces you closer to your value.
  2. Vickrey (1961) won a Nobel for this; the VCG (Vickrey–Clarke–Groves) mechanism extends "pay the harm you impose on others" to complex multi-item settings. Modern ad auctions descend from these ideas.
