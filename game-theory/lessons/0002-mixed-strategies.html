Mixed Strategies & the Indifference Principle

What to do when the underline method finds no equilibrium: randomize — and compute exactly how much.

Last lesson, a matrix with zero double-underlined cells was a loose end. Here's why it happens and what rational players actually do: they stop being predictable. This lesson gives you the second core skill — computing the mixed-strategy equilibrium of a 2×2 game by hand.

Why pure equilibria can fail: be unpredictable

Some games punish predictability. If your rival can guess your move, they exploit it — so any pure choice invites a counter, and best responses chase each other in a circle with nowhere to rest.1 The classic bare-bones version is Matching Pennies: you win by mismatching, they win by matching. Whatever you'd reliably pick, they'd pick to beat.

Matching Pennies — Row wins on a mismatch, Column on a match (zero-sum)
Col: HeadsCol: Tails
Row: Heads(−1, 1)(1, −1)
Row: Tails(1, −1)(−1, 1)

Run the underline passes and the best responses never coincide — no pure NE. The resolution: a mixed strategy, a probability over your actions. Here, obviously, flip 50/50 so you can't be read. But "obvious" only works when the game is symmetric. For everything else, you need the method.

The method: make your opponent indifferent

Here is the move that surprises everyone the first time:

The indifference principle In a mixed equilibrium, you choose your mix so that your opponent is indifferent between the actions they're mixing over.

Why: if your opponent strictly preferred one of their actions, they'd play it for sure — and then randomizing is a mistake, they'd exploit you. Randomizing is only rational when both of your actions pay equally. So each of you tunes your dice to flatten the other's incentives.

Concretely, for a 2×2: write down the opponent's expected payoff to each of their two actions as a function of your mixing probability, then set those two expressions equal and solve. That single equation gives your equilibrium mix.

Worked example — an audit game (compliance)

Straight from the mission. An Auditor decides each period to Inspect (costly) or Trust. A Trader decides to trade Honest or Cheat. Cheating pays — unless you're inspected and caught.

Auditor (rows) vs. Trader (columns) — payoffs = (Auditor, Trader)
Trader: HonestTrader: Cheat
Auditor: Inspect(−1, 1)(2, −2)
Auditor: Trust(0, 1)(−2, 2)

First confirm there's no pure NE. Auditor best-responds Trust to Honest but Inspect to Cheat; Trader best-responds Honest to Inspect but Cheat to Trust. The arrows chase in a circle — no double-underline. Good, we need a mix.

Step 1 — the Auditor's mix (makes the Trader indifferent)

Let the Auditor Inspect with probability p. The Trader's expected payoffs:

  • Honest: p·(1) + (1−p)·(1) = 1  (honest always pays 1)
  • Cheat: p·(−2) + (1−p)·(2) = 2 − 4p

Set equal: 1 = 2 − 4p ⟹ p = 1/4. The Auditor inspects a quarter of the time — just enough that cheating no longer pays.

Step 2 — the Trader's mix (makes the Auditor indifferent)

Let the Trader be Honest with probability q. The Auditor's expected payoffs:

  • Inspect: q·(−1) + (1−q)·(2) = 2 − 3q
  • Trust: q·(0) + (1−q)·(−2) = −2 + 2q

Set equal: 2 − 3q = −2 + 2q ⟹ 5q = 4 ⟹ q = 4/5. The Trader is honest 4/5 of the time and cheats 1/5.

The equilibrium: Auditor inspects 1/4, Trader cheats 1/5. Neither can do better by changing their dice.

The twist worth remembering

Counterintuitive — and deeply useful Look again: the Auditor's mix p fell out of the Trader's payoffs, and the Trader's mix q fell out of the Auditor's payoffs.

Your equilibrium probabilities are pinned down by the other player's incentives, not your own. So if you raise the punishment for cheating, you don't change how often the auditor needs to inspect in the naïve way — you change how often the trader cheats. This inversion is the doorway to mechanism design: to move someone's behavior, change the payoffs they face, not your own.

Check yourself

Q1. In a mixed equilibrium, whose payoffs determine your mixing probabilities?

Your opponent's — you mix precisely to make them indifferent between actions.

If they weren't indifferent they'd go pure and exploit any tilt in your dice.

Q2. You compute a "probability" of 1.3 for some action. What went wrong — or what does it mean?

No interior mix exists; a pure strategy is the best response instead there.

Probabilities outside [0,1] signal the game actually has a pure-strategy solution.

Q3. (Spaced recall from Lesson 1) Before mixing, how do you check a 2×2 has no pure NE?

Underline each player's best payoff; no cell gets both numbers underlined.

Always check for pure equilibria first — only reach for mixing when none exist.

Now you drive

Try it — compute the mix before revealing

A Defender guards one of two sites; an Attacker raids one. The south site is more valuable. Payoffs = (Defender, Attacker), zero-sum. Find the probability the Defender should guard North so the Attacker can't exploit either raid.

Attacker: Raid NAttacker: Raid S
Defender: Guard N(1, −1)(−2, 2)
Defender: Guard S(−1, 1)(1, −1)
Reveal the solution

Guard North with probability 2/5 (so guard South 3/5).

Let the Defender guard North with prob g. Make the Attacker indifferent: Raid N pays the attacker g·(−1) + (1−g)·(1) = 1 − 2g; Raid S pays g·(2) + (1−g)·(−1) = 3g − 1. Set equal: 1 − 2g = 3g − 1 ⟹ 5g = 2 ⟹ g = 2/5. Sensible: the more valuable South is guarded more (3/5). The attacker's own mix, by symmetry of the method, comes from the Defender's payoffs.
