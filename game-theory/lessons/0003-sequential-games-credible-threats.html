Sequential Games & Credible Threats

When players move in order, a matrix hides the timing. Solve the tree backward — and learn which threats are bluffs.

So far everyone moved at once. But most strategic life is sequential: someone acts, the other sees it and responds. Timing changes everything — and it exposes the single most useful idea in strategy: a threat only works if you'd actually carry it out. This lesson gives you backward induction, the tool that separates real threats from bluffs.

A new picture: the game tree

Sequential games are drawn as an extensive form — a tree. Nodes are decision points (labeled with who moves); branches are actions; the leaves hold the payoff pair. Reading order is left-to-right in time.

The method: backward induction

Backward induction Start at the last decisions. At each, assume that player picks their best payoff — prune the branches they'd never take. Replace that node with the payoff of the surviving branch, then step back to the previous decision and repeat. Work all the way to the root. What survives is the subgame-perfect equilibrium (SPE): the prediction where every player is optimal at every point they might actually move.

The trick is the assumption baked into each step: whoever moves later will do what's best for them when the moment comes — not what they promised earlier. That's what kills bluffs.

Worked example — entry deterrence

Straight from the mission. An Entrant decides whether to enter a market an Incumbent currently owns. If they enter, the Incumbent chooses to Accommodate (share the market) or Fight (a price war that bloodies both).

Green = the branch each player actually chooses · dashed = pruned. Payoffs = (Entrant, Incumbent).

Step 1 — the last mover (Incumbent), if entry happens. Accommodate pays 2, Fight pays −1. The Incumbent accommodates. Prune "Fight."

Step 2 — fold back to the Entrant. The Entrant now knows entry leads to Accommodate → payoff 2, versus staying Out → 0. So they enter.

SPE: Entrant enters, Incumbent accommodates → (2, 2).

The payoff: spotting a non-credible threat

Now write that same game as a Lesson-1 matrix and underline best responses:

The entry game in normal form — (Entrant, Incumbent)
Incumbent: FightIncumbent: Accommodate
Entrant: In(−1, −1)(2, 2)
Entrant: Out(0, 4)(0, 4)

There are two pure Nash equilibria: (In, Accommodate) and (Out, Fight). The second says: the Entrant stays out because the Incumbent threatens to fight. It's a valid Nash equilibrium — yet backward induction throws it away. Why?

Credible vs. non-credible threats "If you enter, I'll fight" is a non-credible threat. If the Entrant actually entered, fighting pays the Incumbent −1 versus +2 to accommodate — so they wouldn't fight. A threat you'd never execute doesn't change a rational rival's behavior. Subgame-perfect equilibrium keeps only the equilibria held up by credible moves. This is exactly why the SPE refinement exists: plain Nash equilibrium is fooled by bluffs; backward induction isn't.

The strategic flip: commitment

Here's the part that pays rent in negotiation and markets. If the Incumbent could make "Fight" credible, the Entrant would stay out and the Incumbent would keep the whole market (payoff 4 > 2). How? Change your own future payoffs before the rival moves — build excess capacity, sign a "we'll match any price" contract, stake your reputation. Each makes fighting cheap or automatic, so the threat becomes real.

Commitment value Counterintuitively, removing your own options can make you better off — because it changes what rivals expect you to do. Burning a bridge, a binding contract, an escrow, a public precommitment: all are moves that win by limiting yourself credibly. (Sequential thinking + commitment is the backbone of bargaining, deterrence, and market microstructure.)1

Check yourself

Q1. What does backward induction assume at every decision node?

Whoever moves there will pick their own best payoff when the moment arrives.

That "when the moment arrives" is what strips out promises they wouldn't keep.

Q2. Why is the (Out, Fight) Nash equilibrium rejected by subgame perfection?

Fighting is a non-credible threat; faced with entry the Incumbent won't fight.

SPE requires optimal play in every subgame, including the one after entry.

Q3. (Spaced recall — Lesson 2) In a mixed equilibrium, whose payoffs fix your mixing probabilities?

Your opponent's — you randomize to leave them indifferent between actions.

Different tool, same discipline: reason about what makes the other player act.

Now you drive

Try it — solve the tree before revealing

A Buyer can place an order (paying a deposit) or not. If they order, the Supplier either delivers or keeps the deposit and reneges. Solve by backward induction: what happens, and why is the good outcome (3, 2) lost?

Payoffs = (Buyer, Supplier).

Reveal the solution

The Buyer doesn't order; outcome (0, 0).

Last mover first: if an order arrives, the Supplier compares Deliver (2) vs Renege (3) → Renege. Fold back: the Buyer foresees that ordering leads to −2, worse than 0 from not ordering → Don't order. The efficient (3, 2) evaporates because the Supplier can't credibly promise to deliver. This is the hold-up problem — and the fix is a commitment device that changes the Supplier's payoff from reneging: an enforceable contract, an escrow, a reputation/repeat-game, a smart contract. Same lesson as entry deterrence, mirror image: there a threat needed credibility; here a promise does.
