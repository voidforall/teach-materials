Accrual Accounting and Earnings Quality

Accounting's core genius — and its core vulnerability — is the same idea: record economic events when they happen, not when money moves. Learn the idea, and you can measure how much of a company's "earnings" you should actually believe.

The accrual principle: revenue is booked when earned (delivered, promised to a paying customer), expenses when matched to the revenue they helped create — regardless of when cash moves. Without it, a company that sells on 90-day terms would look profit-less for a quarter, then falsely rich the next. Accrual accounting is right in principle.

But every accrual is a judgment about the future. Will the customer pay? Will the software deal renew? Is that inventory worth its cost? Manipulation lives exactly here — in the gap between what was booked and what cash eventually arrived.

The accruals gap: your earnings-quality meter

Accruals = net income − operating cash flow. Some gap is normal (growing receivables in a growing business). But large, persistent, positive accruals mean profit is running ahead of cash — and that is the single most reliable signal of trouble. Richard Sloan's famous 1996 study ("Do Stock Prices Fully Reflect Information in Accruals and Cash Flows?") showed companies with high accruals systematically disappoint — the market believes the earnings that cash does not. Run the gap over three years, not one: a single quarter means nothing; a trend means everything.

Judging quality, not just quantity

  • High-quality earnings: cash-backed (OCF ≈ or > NI), recurring (from the core business, not asset sales or tax quirks), conservatively recognized (bad news recognized early).
  • Low-quality earnings: driven by one-time gains, aggressive revenue timing, capitalized costs, or serial "restructuring" charges.
  • Non-GAAP "adjusted" earnings: sometimes a legitimate cleaner view, often a vanity metric. The tell: adjustments that always raise earnings — stock-based compensation being "added back" every single year, as if employees worked for free. Compare GAAP to non-GAAP; the size of the bridge is the size of the sales job.

Buffett has spent decades on this theme in his shareholder letters: respect accounting, but never mistake its output for economic truth — earnings are real only to the extent they eventually become cash.

Keystone Earnings are a hypothesis; cash is the experiment. Every income statement is a set of claims about the future. Track net income against operating cash flow for three years and you are running the experiment that tests the hypothesis.
Anti-pattern Treating "adjusted EBITDA" as earnings. It is neither earnings (ignores taxes and often real costs) nor cash flow (ignores working capital and capex). Debt contracts use it because lenders define it precisely; managements quote it because it is always bigger. Use it only with its GAAP-to-bridge attached.

Retrieve it

State the accrual principle in one sentence.

Record events when economically real.

Revenue when earned, expenses when matched to the revenue they produce — not when cash happens to move. Correct in principle; exploitable through estimates.

How do you compute the accruals gap?

Net income minus operating cash.

The remainder is profit booked ahead of cash — receivables, inventory, capitalized costs. Persistent large positives predict disappointment (Sloan, 1996).

What reveals a self-serving non-GAAP measure?

Adjustments that only ever help.

Stock comp added back every year, "one-time" items recurring annually. If the GAAP-to-non-GAAP bridge only ever points up, you are reading marketing, not accounting.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 15 minutes For a company you follow, compute net income − OCF for the last three years from EDGAR. Shrinking gap: earnings are converting to cash — quality. Widening gap: write down the three most likely explanations (receivables? inventory? capitalized costs?) and check the balance sheet to see which one it is.

Primary source

Buffett's shareholder letters — search them for "earnings quality" and "owner earnings"; decades of the same warning, beautifully argued.
