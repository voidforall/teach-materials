The Investor's Ratios: Margins, ROE, and DuPont

Raw numbers don't travel — a $2bn profit means nothing without context. Ratios are the compression algorithm: they turn three statements into a handful of comparable signals. Each one has a blind spot, and today you learn both.

Ratios only mean something compared to something: the same company over time (trend), its industry peers (position), or its own past guidance (credibility). A 12% net margin is mediocre for software and spectacular for a grocery chain. Never quote a ratio without saying compared to what.

Profitability: the margin ladder

  • Gross margin = gross profit / revenue. Pricing power and production economics. Stable-or-rising over years is a moat signal; eroding gross margin means competition has arrived.
  • Operating margin = EBIT / revenue. Adds SG&A and R&D — how efficiently the whole business runs. Compare within industry only.
  • Net margin = net income / revenue. Polluted by interest and taxes; useful mainly for the trend.

ROE and the DuPont decomposition

Return on equity — net income / shareholders' equity — is the single most-quoted ratio, and the most dangerous. DuPont splits it into three engines:

ROE = net margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier
(net income/revenue) × (revenue/assets) × (assets/equity)

Three very different companies can post 15% ROE: a luxury of margin (software), a machine of turnover (retail), or a pile of leverage (a bank-adjacent balance sheet). Only the first two create value; the third rents it at a price. Damodaran's lecture notes treat this decomposition as the entry ticket to serious analysis — and it's the on-ramp to ROIC in the valuation course.

Liquidity and solvency

  • Current ratio = current assets / current liabilities. Can it pay this year's bills? Around 1.5–2 is comfortable; below 1 needs a story.
  • Quick ratio: same but excludes inventory — the test if sales stopped tomorrow.
  • Debt/equity and interest coverage = EBIT / interest expense. Solvency isn't about debt size but whether operating profit comfortably services it.
Keystone A ratio is a question, not an answer. Each one compresses three statements into one number — and every compression loses something. The DuPont decomposition matters because it refuses to let one happy number (ROE) hide three different stories about how it was earned.
Anti-pattern Screening on ROE alone. Buybacks shrink equity, mechanically inflating ROE for a company with flat profits; leverage does the same with added risk. A high ROE you haven't decomposed is a headline, not a finding.

Retrieve it

Three companies each post 15% ROE. Name their three engines.

Margin, turnover, leverage.

DuPont: net margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier. High margin means pricing power; high turnover means asset efficiency; high leverage means risk rented from creditors.

Buybacks happen; net income is flat. What happens to ROE?

ROE mechanically rises.

Buybacks reduce shareholders' equity, shrinking the denominator. The business didn't improve — the arithmetic did. Decompose before celebrating.

Which margin best signals a durable moat?

Stable gross margin.

Gross margin isolates pricing power from overhead choices. A moat that holds for years shows up as gross margin that refuses to erode while competitors discount.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 15 minutes Pick a company on EDGAR. From its last annual statements, compute the three DuPont components and the current ratio. Write one sentence per component: is this ROE built on quality (margin, turnover) or on leverage? Then compare each margin to a direct competitor — the industry-only rule in action.
