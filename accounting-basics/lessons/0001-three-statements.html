The Three Statements

Every company you can invest in files three statements that fit together like gears. Learn what each one reports and how they lock together, and you can pick up any earnings release in the world.

Accounting looks intimidating because it's taught as bookkeeping. Investors don't need that. You need three documents, each answering one question about a period of time (a quarter, a year):

  • Income statement — Did we make a profit? Revenue minus expenses = net income.
  • Cash flow statement — Where did the cash go? Opening cash + flows = closing cash.
  • Balance sheet — What are we worth on paper? A snapshot, not a period: assets = liabilities + equity.

Two are home movies (they cover a period); one is a photograph (one instant, usually the last day of the period). Confusing a flow with a stock is the beginner's central error — profit is a flow, cash in the bank is a stock.

How they lock together

The three statements articulate — accountants' word for "must reconcile":

  • Net income from the income statement flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet (minus dividends).
  • The cash flow statement starts from net income and ends at cash — the very cash sitting at the top of the balance sheet.
  • Depreciation appears on the income statement (expense), the cash flow statement (added back), and the balance sheet (reducing asset values) simultaneously.

Because they must reconcile, a lie in one statement must be papered over in another. That's why experienced investors read all three at once — the seams are where the truth leaks out.

Keystone Income statement: performance over time. Cash flow: the same period in cash. Balance sheet: one day's snapshot. They must reconcile. If a company reports soaring profits while cash stagnates, the articulation tells you where the gap went — usually receivables or inventory piling up on the balance sheet.
Anti-pattern Reading only the income statement. "Earnings are up 30%" is the headline, not the story. Enron reported gorgeous profits right up to bankruptcy; its cash flow statement had been screaming for years. Always triangulate.

Retrieve it

Which statement is a snapshot, not a period?

The balance sheet.

It reports assets, liabilities and equity at one instant — typically the fiscal year-end. Income and cash flow statements cover a stretch of time.

Profits rise, but cash doesn't. Where did the money go?

Into working capital.

Booked-but-uncollected sales become accounts receivable; unsold goods become inventory. Both are balance-sheet assets, not cash — the classic accrual gap that Lessons 4–5 turn into a diagnostic.

Why must the three statements reconcile?

Double-entry bookkeeping forces it.

Every transaction touches at least two accounts. Net income has to land in equity; ending cash has to equal the balance sheet's cash line. Reconciliation is your free audit trail.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 10 minutes Open the SEC's own guided example, How to Read a 10-K, and scroll to the financial statements of the sample company. Find: (1) net income on the income statement, (2) the same number starting the cash flow statement, (3) retained earnings on the balance sheet. You've just traced the articulation with your own eyes.
