The Balance Sheet: What It Owns, Owes, and Is Worth

One equation — assets = liabilities + equity — and one instant in time. The balance sheet is a photograph, not a movie, and for investors it's where the income statement's claims get checked: the receivables, the inventory, the debt that profit either produced or papered over.

Everything on this statement hangs off one identity that must always hold:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity. What the company owns (left side) was paid for either by someone it owes (liabilities) or by its owners (equity). Rearranged: equity = assets − liabilities — the residual left for shareholders if everything were settled at book value.

Unlike the income statement, this is a snapshot: it describes one day, usually the fiscal year-end. The left side tells you what the business runs on; the right side tells you who financed it — creditors first, you second.

Touring the left side: assets

  • Current assets (convertible to cash within a year): cash, marketable securities, accounts receivable (money customers owe you), inventory.
  • Non-current assets: property, plant & equipment (PP&E, carried at cost minus depreciation); goodwill and intangibles — what the company paid above fair value in acquisitions. Goodwill is the most investor-suspicious line on the page: it's a paid-for-hope that gets written down years later when the hope dies. A big goodwill balance means acquisitions, not necessarily assets.

Touring the right side: liabilities and equity

  • Current liabilities (due within a year): payables, short-term debt, and deferred revenue — cash collected before the work is done. It's a liability only in name: customers financing the business is a good kind of owing.
  • Long-term debt: bonds and loans; check the maturity schedule in the notes — a wall of debt maturing in one bad year is how good companies die.
  • Equity: paid-in capital (what shareholders put in, including new issuance — dilution) vs retained earnings (accumulated profits kept in the business — the good kind of growth).

The investor lens

  • Working capital = current assets − current liabilities: the cushion for paying near-term bills.
  • Red flag preview: receivables or inventory growing much faster than revenue means sales are being booked but not converted to cash — the accrual gap from Lesson 1, now with a location.
  • Equity growth quality: equity rising via retained earnings compounds your stake; equity rising via repeated share issuance splits your stake.
  • Debt maturity structure: not just how much debt, but when it comes due and at what rates.
Keystone Assets = liabilities + equity, on one day. Equity is a residual, not a valuation: it's what's left after book-value accounting nets assets against debts. The investor's questions are always: are the assets real and collectible, is the debt survivable, and is equity growing from profits or from printing shares?
Anti-pattern Treating book equity as the company's value. Book equity is an accounting residual — historical cost with depreciation guesses, plus whatever goodwill management overpaid for. The market value of a company is what future cash flows are worth, not what the ledger says is left over (Damodaran's valuation lectures cover the gap in depth). Some wonderful companies trade at many times book; some worthless ones have huge book value made of goodwill.

Retrieve it

Why is deferred revenue called a "good" liability?

Customers paid before the work was done.

The company already holds the cash; the "owing" is delivery of a product or service, not money. It means customers finance operations upfront — a sign of pricing power and demand, common in software and subscriptions.

Receivables are growing twice as fast as revenue. What should you suspect?

Sales are booked but not collected.

The accrual gap is widening: revenue is recognized on paper while cash waits in receivables. It can be loose credit terms or aggressive revenue recognition — either way it's the classic early red flag, and Lesson 7 turns it into a full diagnostic.

Which is the most investor-suspicious asset line, and why?

Goodwill.

It's the premium paid over fair value in acquisitions — a record of past optimism, not a usable asset. It produces nothing and tends to be written down in lumps when acquisitions disappoint, which is management quietly admitting the overpayment.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 15 minutes On SEC EDGAR, pull the latest 10-K of a company you know and find its balance sheet (the SEC's How to Read a 10-K walks you there). Compute the current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities. Under 1 means short-term liabilities exceed short-term assets — not automatically fatal, but you'd better check the cash line and debt maturities. Then compare retained earnings vs paid-in capital: is equity growing from profits or from issuing shares?
