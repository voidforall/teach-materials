Capstone: Read a Real 10-K in One Hour

The 10-K is the full annual report every US-listed company must file with the SEC. It runs to hundreds of pages — but with the right reading order, an investor extracts what matters in about an hour. This is the course's final move.

Where to find it: SEC EDGAR full-text search — type the company name, filter by form type "10-K", open the latest filing. Non-US filers (Alibaba, Toyota) file a 20-F instead; unlisted companies publish annual reports with the same anatomy. Don't confuse the 8-K earnings release — a short, management-polished press release — with the 10-K, which contains the audited statements and the footnotes. The press release is the trailer; the 10-K is the film.

The one-hour reading order

  1. Business section & risk factors (~15 min) — how the company actually makes money, and what management admits can kill it. Risks listed first are usually the real ones.
  2. MD&A (~15 min) — management's discussion and analysis: their story about the year. Read it critically and keep it — when next year's results differ, the gap between story and outcome is the lesson.
  3. Financial statements, cash-first (~20 min) — cash flow statement first (the hardest to fake), then the income statement, then the balance sheet. This is Lesson 1's order of trust, inverted from the filing's order.
  4. The footnotes that matter most (~10 min): revenue recognition (when does a sale count?), segment data (which business actually earns the money?), debt maturities (when does the wall of repayment hit?), stock compensation (the real cost of employees), and contingent liabilities (lawsuits and promises that might become bills).

The printable checklist: 10 things to extract

  • Revenue growth — and whether price or volume drove it (Lesson 2: income statement).
  • Margin trend — gross, operating, net, over 3+ years (Lessons 2 and 6: ratios).
  • OCF vs net income — OCF ≥ NI is health (Lesson 5: earnings quality).
  • Free cash flow = OCF − capex (Lesson 4: cash flow).
  • Debt level and maturity schedule — can it be refinanced on sane terms? (Lesson 3: balance sheet).
  • DSO and inventory vs revenue growth — receivables outgrowing sales is the classic tell (Lesson 7: red flags).
  • Share count trend — dilution via stock comp vs buybacks; check the diluted EPS line (Lesson 2).
  • Auditor opinion — anything other than an unqualified opinion is a stop sign (Lesson 7).
  • Related-party transactions — in the footnotes; arm's-length businesses rarely need them (Lesson 7).
  • GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap — how far "adjusted" earnings sit from the audited number (Lesson 5).

Every item maps back to a lesson from this course — 0001 through 0007 — which is the point: the course was always building toward this hour.

Keystone Story first, cash second, footnotes always. The one-hour order works because it front-loads the qualitative (what can kill it), trusts the hardest-to-fake number (cash flow), and reserves explicit time for the footnotes, where the disclosures management hopes you'll skim actually live.
Anti-pattern Skipping the footnotes. The financial statements are the summary; the footnotes are the source. Revenue-recognition changes, debt covenants, pending litigation, and related-party deals all hide there — that's where the bodies are buried. A 10-K read without footnotes is a 10-K half-read.

Retrieve it

Which document would a German company listed in Frankfurt file with the SEC?

A Form 20-F.

Non-US filers with US-listed securities file the 20-F annually instead of the 10-K. It has the same anatomy — business, risks, MD&A-equivalent, audited statements, footnotes — under IFRS rather than US GAAP.

Why read the cash flow statement before the income statement?

Because cash is harder to fake.

Profit is built from estimates — accruals, provisions, recognition timing. Operating cash flow is bounded by bank balances. Reading it first anchors you in the fact before you meet the opinion.

Which footnote tells you when the repayment wall hits?

The debt maturities schedule.

The balance sheet shows total debt; the footnote breaks it into years. A big tower maturing into a weak credit market has sunk otherwise viable companies — maturity timing matters as much as amount.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 1 hour Pick any US-listed company. Get its latest 10-K from EDGAR, run the one-hour reading order and the 10-item checklist above, and write a one-paragraph verdict: would you invest, and what single number made the difference? Then hand the paragraph to your teacher-agent for critique.
