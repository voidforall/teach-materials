The Income Statement: From Revenue to Net Income

One page, one ladder: revenue at the top, net income and EPS at the bottom, and a series of subtraction steps in between. Learn the ladder once and every income statement in the world becomes readable — including the parts management would rather you skim past.

The income statement covers a period — a quarter or a year — and reads like a waterfall. Each line narrows the profit:

  • Revenue — what customers were billed.
  • − Cost of goods sold (COGS) — the direct cost of what was sold.
  • = Gross profit — what's left to run everything else.
  • − Operating expenses — selling, general & administrative (SG&A), R&D, and depreciation & amortization (D&A).
  • = Operating income (EBIT) — profit from the actual business.
  • − Interest — the cost of debt (or + interest earned on cash).
  • = Pre-tax income → − tax = Net income → ÷ shares = EPS.

Basic vs diluted EPS

Basic EPS is net income divided by shares outstanding today. Diluted EPS also counts shares that could exist — stock options, restricted stock units, convertible bonds. Investors quote diluted because it's the honest version: your slice of the pie after everyone who's been promised a slice shows up. A company can grow diluted EPS barely at all while basic EPS soars — that gap is dilution, and it's coming out of your pocket.

Hard lines vs judgment calls

Not every line is equally trustworthy. Some are close to cash:

  • Hard: revenue backed by invoiced sales, interest paid, cash taxes (mostly), COGS for simple businesses.
  • Judgment calls: revenue recognition timing (booked now, delivered later — software and construction are notorious); D&A estimates (management picks useful lives and residual values); one-time items ("restructuring," "impairment" — are they really one-time?); stock-based compensation (a real cost to you via dilution, though it never touches cash).

The higher the proportion of judgment in a company's earnings, the more you should discount the headline and check the cash flow statement (Lesson 4).

Margins as business quality

Gross margin (gross profit ÷ revenue) measures pricing power: can the company charge more than its product costs? Operating margin (operating income ÷ revenue) measures whether that pricing power survives the cost of running the company. A fat gross margin with a thin operating margin means SG&A is eating the franchise. A thin gross margin that's stable over years (think a well-run retailer or grocer) can still be a fine business — the quality signal is stability and trend, not the absolute level.

Keystone The income statement is a ladder of subtractions: revenue → gross profit → operating income → net income → EPS. The rungs between them — gross margin and operating margin — are the business-quality signals; and the closer a line sits to cash, the harder it is to fake.
Anti-pattern Obsessing over the EPS headline. EPS can grow because the business got better — or because of a one-time tax benefit, or because the company bought back shares (same earnings, fewer shares). Always ask what produced the EPS growth: revenue? margin? or just the share count and the tax line? Buyback-driven EPS growth with flat revenue is financial engineering, not performance.

Retrieve it

Gross profit minus operating expenses leaves you at which line?

Operating income (EBIT).

The ladder runs revenue − COGS = gross profit; gross profit − operating expenses (SG&A, R&D, D&A) = operating income. Interest and tax come after, so EBIT shows the business itself, independent of how it's financed.

Why do investors quote diluted rather than basic EPS?

Diluted counts shares that could exist.

Options, RSUs and convertibles all turn into real shares eventually. Diluted EPS prices that in today, so it's the conservative, honest measure of your per-share claim on earnings.

Gross margin is fat but operating margin is thin. What does that tell you?

Operating costs are eating the pricing power.

The product commands a premium (high gross margin), but SG&A or R&D consumes it before operating income. The problem is overhead or spending discipline, not the product's economics.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 15 minutes Pick a company you know on SEC EDGAR, open its latest 10-K, and find the income statement (the SEC's How to Read a 10-K shows you where). Walk the whole ladder: revenue, COGS, gross profit, operating income, net income, basic and diluted EPS. Then compute gross margin and operating margin, and check the diluted-vs-basic gap — that's the dilution shareholders paid for.
