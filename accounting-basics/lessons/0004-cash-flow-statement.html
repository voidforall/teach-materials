The Cash Flow Statement: Earnings vs Actual Cash

The income statement is what the company says happened; the cash flow statement is what happened to the money. Investors who read only one of them are reading half the story — and usually the flattering half.

The cash flow statement tracks every dollar in and out over the period, sorted into three buckets:

  • Operating (OCF) — cash generated by the business itself: customer payments, wages, rent, suppliers, taxes. It starts from net income and adds back non-cash items (depreciation, stock comp), then subtracts the cash swallowed by growing receivables and inventory.
  • Investing — capital expenditure, acquisitions, asset sales. Mostly cash out for a healthy grower.
  • Financing — debt raised and repaid, dividends paid, shares bought back or issued. The shareholder-relations bucket.

By construction it ends at the change in cash, which must equal the cash line on the balance sheet — Lesson 1's articulation doing its job.

Free cash flow: the investor's favorite line

FCF = operating cash flow − capital expenditure. This is the money actually available to reward owners: pay dividends, buy back shares, repay debt — without borrowing or selling equity. It is also the raw material of every DCF in the valuation course. A company can report any profit it likes; FCF is far harder to fake, because fake cash bounces.

Why profit ≠ cash

Two forces drive the wedge. Non-cash charges: depreciation lowers profit but not cash (the cash left when the machine was bought); stock comp does the same. Working capital: growth consumes cash — every new receivable is profit you booked but can't spend. The rule of thumb worth memorizing: over any multi-year stretch, OCF should roughly match or exceed net income. A persistent gap in the wrong direction is the first red flag of Lesson 7.

Buffett made the same point with his "owner earnings" concept in his 1986 shareholder letter: reported profit must be adjusted for depreciation, amortization and the capex actually required to keep the machine running — which is just FCF argued carefully.

Keystone Revenue is an opinion; cash is a fact. The income statement bends around estimates and timing choices. The cash flow statement bends around reality. When they disagree for years, the gap itself is the finding.
Anti-pattern Calling a business "cash-generative" off OCF alone. An airline can post healthy operating cash flow every year and still destroy value, because the investing section bleeds it all back out in aircraft capex. OCF is the harvest; FCF is what's left after replanting the fields. Always net out capex.

Retrieve it

Name the statement's three sections.

Operating, investing, financing.

Business cash, investment cash, shareholder-and-creditor cash. Their sum must equal the period's change in the balance-sheet cash line.

Depreciation is added back — why?

It isn't current cash.

The cash left when the asset was purchased, years ago, in the investing section. Depreciation reduces profit now without moving a dollar today, so OCF adds it back.

Define free cash flow precisely.

Operating cash minus capex.

That residual is what can fund dividends, buybacks and debt paydown without borrowing — and it's the input every DCF valuation is built on.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 10 minutes Pick any company on EDGAR. From last year's cash flow statement, write down OCF, capex, and compute FCF. Then compare FCF to net income and to dividends + buybacks: is the company funding shareholder returns from real cash, from borrowing, or not at all?

Primary source

Buffett's 1986 letter — the owner-earnings passage is the best two paragraphs ever written on why cash and profit differ.
