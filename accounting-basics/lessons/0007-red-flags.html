Red Flags: How Earnings Get Manipulated

Manipulation is not infinite creativity — it's a small bag of tricks used over and over. Learn the bag, and the checklist that catches it, and you'll recognize the next scandal years before the headline.

Howard Schilit's Financial Shenanigans catalogs the tricks into families. Investors need three:

  • Revenue games — recognizing revenue early or recognizing revenue that isn't real: channel stuffing (shipping distributors more than they can sell), recording multi-year contracts up front, and outright fabrication (Luckin Coffee invented sales).
  • Expense games — making costs disappear: capitalizing what should be an operating expense (WorldCom capitalized ordinary line costs, inflating profits by billions), stretching depreciation, and "cookie-jar" reserves saved in good years to release in bad ones.
  • Degree-of-seriousness games — everything is technically disclosed but presented to mislead: serial "one-time" charges, non-GAAP earnings that always add back stock comp, and bad news buried in footnotes.

The detective checklist

Each trick leaves fingerprints in the statements, because — Lesson 1 — they must reconcile. Run this on any company you own:

  • Receivables growing much faster than revenue — sales booked that customers haven't paid for (or don't exist).
  • DSO rising (receivables / (revenue/365)) — the same signal, quantified.
  • Inventory outpacing revenue — demand softening or costs being stashed in the balance sheet.
  • OCF persistently below net income — the accruals gap of Lesson 5, sustained for years, is the single best fraud predictor.
  • Serial "one-time" items — if it happens every year, it's a cost of doing business.
  • Wide GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap — the bigger the bridge from GAAP EPS to "adjusted" EPS, the harder someone is working to sell you a number.
  • Governance tells — auditor resignations or changes, frequent CFO turnover, related-party transactions, restatements.

One sentence each for the classics: Enron used mark-to-market accounting and off-balance-sheet entities to book imaginary future profits; WorldCom simply treated operating expenses as capital investments; Luckin invented a large share of its China sales. Every one of them tripped multiple checklist items for years.

Keystone When the story and the cash flows disagree, believe the cash. Profit is an opinion shaped by estimates; cash is a fact. A company whose profits rise while operating cash flow stagnates is telling you two stories — pick the boring one.
Anti-pattern "It's audited, so it's safe." Auditors give reasonable assurance against material misstatement — they catch errors far more often than determined fraud, and management chooses the auditor. Enron's auditor (Arthur Andersen) signed off to the end. The audit opinion is a data point, not a shield.

Retrieve it

WorldCom's core trick, in one phrase?

Capitalizing operating expenses.

Ordinary line costs were booked as capital investments — leaving the income statement for the balance sheet — inflating profit by billions until the reversal crushed it.

Which single metric best predicts trouble?

Operating cash flow lagging income.

Faked revenue creates receivables, not cash. A multi-year gap between net income and OCF is the fingerprint shared by nearly every earnings-manipulation case.

Cookie-jar reserves: how do they work?

Over-provision now, release later.

In good years management stuffs excess provisions into the jar; in bad years releasing them props up earnings — smoothing results into a lie about volatility.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 20 minutes Take a company you own or follow. Pull three years from EDGAR and run all seven checklist items. Write down which flags trip, even faintly. No flags is a finding too — record it, and re-run the checklist every year when the 10-K lands.
