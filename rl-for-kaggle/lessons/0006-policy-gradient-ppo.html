Policy Gradient Methods: PPO

Instead of scoring actions, learn the policy directly: push up the probability of actions that worked better than expected. PPO is that idea with a clip that stops each update from overcommitting.

The baseline algorithm is REINFORCE: sample a rollout, then nudge log π(a|s) in proportion to the return. Its problem is variance — a few lucky episodes dominate the gradient. The fix chain, each step cutting variance or bias:

  • Advantage A(s,a) = Q − V: subtract a learned baseline so only the relative goodness of an action drives the update.
  • GAE(λ): blend TD errors across time horizons to estimate advantage with low variance and tolerable bias.
  • PPO's clipped objective (Schulman et al., 2017): the trust-region idea without the second-order math.
L = −E[ min( r(θ)·A,  clip(r(θ), 1−ε, 1+ε)·A ) ]  −  c_ent · H(π)
where r(θ) = π_θ(a|s) / π_θ_old(a|s)

Read the clip as: if the new policy has already moved probability ratio beyond 1±ε, further improvement in that direction earns zero gradient — the update is saturated, not rewarded. You typically run a few epochs of minibatch SGD over each rollout, then discard it (PPO is on-policy), plus an entropy bonus so the policy doesn't collapse to determinism before it's good.

Why PPO owns self-play on Kaggle

Value-based methods need a replay buffer; in self-play, old transitions come from matches against stale opponents, which poisons off-policy learning unless you're careful. PPO is on-policy: it always trains on fresh games against the current ladder of snapshots, which is exactly how you want self-play to work. It's also forgiving — mediocre hyperparameters still learn. That's why the Lux AI winners used PPO-based pipelines (usually via Stable-Baselines3) on top of a gym-wrapped simulator. Typical starting points: n_steps=2048, n_epochs=10, clip_range=0.2, ent_coef=0.01, gae_lambda=0.95, many parallel envs.

Keystone PPO = policy gradient + advantage (GAE) + a clip that caps how far one update may move the policy. It dominates Kaggle self-play because on-policy training always matches the current opponent pool, and it degrades gracefully when your hyperparameters are merely okay.
Anti-pattern Debugging with the loss curve. PPO's loss is a clipped, entropy-augmented surrogate — it can go up while play gets better and down while play collapses. Watch episode win-rate against a fixed opponent pool and entropy of the policy. A second cousin: reusing old rollouts after policy updates (breaking on-policyness) and wondering why nothing improves.

Retrieve it

What does subtracting the baseline V(s) do to the policy gradient?

Cuts variance, keeps direction.

The expectation of the gradient is unchanged (baseline doesn't depend on the action), but returns centered on V(s) shrink the magnitude of noise across episodes — REINFORCE's core weakness. The centered quantity is the advantage.

In the clipped objective, what happens when r(θ) exceeds 1+ε with positive advantage?

The gradient goes to zero.

The clip saturates: min picks the clipped term, whose gradient with respect to θ is zero. The update is capped — the policy cannot chase one lucky action into a huge probability shift within a single update.

Why is on-policy PPO a natural fit for self-play, where DQN-style replay is awkward?

Opponents keep changing.

Buffered transitions come from play against old snapshots of the opponent, so their Q-targets are stale — off-policy data of the worst kind. PPO discards rollouts after a few epochs and always trains against the current pool, matching how self-play actually evolves.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 15–25 minutes, run then break it With pip install "stable-baselines3[extra]", run SB3 PPO on CartPole-v1 for ~100k steps and plot it (evaluate_policy every 10k steps). Then break it three ways, one at a time, re-running briefly each time: (1) clip_range=10.0; (2) ent_coef=0 and long training; (3) n_epochs=100. Note which failure each change produces (instability, premature determinism, overfitting the rollout). Compare against the Spinning Up PPO page and the SB3 PPO docs.

Primary source

Schulman et al., Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms (2017) — short paper; read Section 2 (the clipped surrogate) carefully, skim the rest. Pair with Spinning Up's PPO page for the derivation chain REINFORCE → GAE → clip.
