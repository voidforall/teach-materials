Self-Play Infrastructure

RL results are won in the harness: the environment wrapper, the simulator speed, and the opponent policy. Build this before you build the learner.

Training needs thousands of episodes per hour; the official runner needs correctness. Use both: wrap the game once, correctly, and reuse the wrapper everywhere.

The Gymnasium seam

Standardize on the Gymnasium API: reset() returns (obs, info), step(action) returns (obs, reward, terminated, truncated, info). Every off-the-shelf library — Stable-Baselines3, CleanRL — plugs into this contract. The wrapper's job:

  • translate obs/config into your state tensor from Lesson 2;
  • apply the action mask for invalid moves;
  • assign rewards at terminals (and shaped rewards along the way, Lesson 4);
  • route the opponent's move — by calling an opponent policy inside step — so the learner always sees one-decision-per-step.

Simulator speed is a hyperparameter

The official kaggle-environments engine is built for fairness and replay, not throughput. Nearly every serious team writes a fast mirror simulator of the rules (vectorized with NumPy, or bit-packed) for training, and validates on the official engine periodically to catch divergence. A 100× faster simulator is a 100× cheaper learning curve — mislabel it as "engineering detail" at your peril.

Self-play and snapshots

Self-play — the current policy playing itself — generates endlessly challenging data. But a policy playing its exact current self chases a moving target and forgets old skills. The standard fix: an opponent pool. Each training run (or interval) saves a snapshot; opponents are sampled from past snapshots, with a bias toward recent ones. This both stabilizes learning and, as Lesson 9 shows, gives you an honest local ladder.

Submission constraints shape the design

No training happens on Kaggle's servers. The submission is inference-only: network weights (pickled or as a file) plus a lean agent() that builds the network, loads weights, and acts — within the per-turn time limit and total file-size budget. Design your checkpoint format for this from day one.

Keystone Wrap once, train fast, submit lean.One Gymnasium wrapper over a fast simulator, opponents drawn from a snapshot pool, and an inference-only submission file — this skeleton carries every algorithm in the rest of the course.
Anti-pattern Training against the current policy only.Latest-vs-latest self-play is a non-stationary chase: strategies cycle (attack/counter-attack) and old strengths are lost. Sample opponents from snapshots of the past to anchor the target.

Retrieve it

What does step return in Gymnasium?

Observation, reward, terminated, truncated, info.

The five-tuple contract every modern RL library expects. Terminated ends the episode by game logic; truncated by a time limit — many algorithms treat them differently.

Why write your own simulator mirror?

Throughput, hence more experience.

The official engine favors correctness and replay over speed. A vectorized mirror multiplies episodes per hour; you periodically cross-check against the official engine to catch rule divergence.

What does the submission file actually contain?

Weights plus inference code.

Kaggle runs no training. The agent builds the network, loads pickled weights, and returns one action per turn within the time budget. Plan this packaging early.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 30–45 minutes Wrap Connect X as a Gymnasium env: opponent routed inside step (start with a random opponent), action mask in info, ±1 terminal reward. Run a random-vs-random loop and measure episodes/second — that number is your training budget for Lessons 5–7.
