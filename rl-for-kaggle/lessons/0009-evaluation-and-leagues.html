Evaluation and Leagues

The public ladder is noisy, slow, and rate-limited — and if you tune against it, it will lie to you. Build your own league at home and treat Kaggle's as the final exam.

Two agents playing 100 games tell you shockingly little: with per-game variance, the standard error of a win-rate estimate is √(p(1−p)/100) ≈ 5%. A 53% local win-rate can be luck. Meanwhile the public ladder runs your submission against a shifting pool of opponents and updates a TrueSkill-like rating slowly, with submission caps (often a few per day). Chasing that number is optimizing a noisy proxy against a non-stationary meta.

The opponent pool

The fix is a fixed, diverse opponent pool you control:

  • Past snapshots of your own policy (every N checkpoints) — prevents forgetting and self-play cycles.
  • Style diversity — scripted bots, the starter agents, aggression- and turtling-tuned variants, plus the strongest public notebooks (where the rules allow).
  • Frozen evaluation protocol — same seeds, same game count, both sides of any asymmetry (swap first-player).

Report win-rate with a confidence interval against the pool, not a single number. A new snapshot is "better" only if the interval against the previous best excludes 50%.

Leagues and ratings

Once you have more than a handful of snapshots, run a league: every agent plays every other, and you maintain Elo or TrueSkill ratings locally. The rating table becomes your training dashboard — you watch the curve of each generation's rating rather than a single win-rate, and you spot pathologies (a new agent that beats the latest snapshot but loses to three older styles is a cycle, not progress). This is a miniature of AlphaStar's league training, and of Kaggle's own ladder mechanics.

# nightly eval loop
for a in candidates:
    for opp in pool:                      # snapshots + scripted + public bots
        wins[a, opp] = play(a, opp, n=200, swap_sides=True)
ratings = trueskill.update_all(wins)
promote(a) if beats(prev_best, CI>50%) and no hard-counter in pool

Guarding against meta-overfit

Two failure modes to design against. Ladder-gaming: specializing to beat the current public meta — a rock that rules this season's scissors — which collapses when the meta shifts (and the final evaluation often uses a fresh agent set). Ladder-chasing: reading too much into small rating moves after each submission; the confidence intervals rarely justify it. Cadence discipline: submit a candidate only when local league evidence says it's better, not to "see what the ladder thinks."

Replays are your debugger

Every ladder and local episode has a replay. When a rating drops, don't retrain blindly — open the losses, find the three positions your agent mishandles, and check whether they're a state-encoding gap (Lesson 2) or a reward issue (Lesson 4). One studied replay is worth a hundred blind PPO reruns.

Keystone Local league with confidence intervals is your ground truth; the public ladder is a noisy, non-stationary sample you check occasionally.Every promotion decision is made against the pool, never against a rating wiggle.
Anti-pattern Evaluating against a single opponent."It beats last week's checkpoint 55%" is one point, not evidence — and agents co-trained against one opponent exploit each other's quirks into a mutual blind spot. Diverse pool, both sides, error bars, or it didn't happen.

Retrieve it

Why are 100 games between two agents often not enough to decide?

The error bar swamps it.

SE of a win-rate at n=100 is about 5 percentage points; a 53-vs-50 difference is well inside noise. Either play many more games or report an interval and withhold judgment.

What does a snapshot pool add that a single sparring partner cannot?

Memory and diversity.

Past snapshots prevent forgetting and self-play cycling; mixed styles expose blind spots a single opponent hides. Together they approximate the real ladder's population.

What is the first debugging step when the public rating drops?

Read the lost replays.

Replays localize the failure — which positions, which phase, which opponent style — turning "rating down" into a concrete encoding or reward bug instead of a blind retraining run.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 30 minutes Take any two Connect X agents from kaggle-environments (e.g. negamax vs your scripted bot). Run 100 games, then 1000, computing the win-rate CI each time. Watch how a "clear" 55% at n=100 becomes "unclear" at n=1000. Then scan a top solution write-up for how the winner structured local evaluation.
