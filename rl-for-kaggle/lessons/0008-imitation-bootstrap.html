Imitation Bootstrap

Self-play from a random policy spends millions of episodes discovering what a decent scripted bot already knows. Clone the bot first, then let RL climb from there.

Every Kaggle simulation ships with something precious: rules-based starter agents, and often a public notebook section full of scripted bots. These are not competitors to beat — they are teachers. Behavioral cloning (BC) turns their trajectories into a supervised dataset: state in, action out, cross-entropy loss. One evening of GPU gives you a policy that plays coherently; self-play from scratch might not get there in a week.

Why clone before you learn

The mechanics: run the scripted expert N episodes, record (obs, expert_action) pairs, train the policy net supervised. Your RL loop then starts from a policy that already opens sensibly, gathers resources, and rarely suicides. The exploration budget goes into refining play, not rediscovering the rules. The Lux AI winners did exactly this — several top solutions pretrained on trajectories generated by the built-from-scratch starter agents (and on stronger public bots), then ran PPO self-play on top.

# bootstrap, once
obs, actions = rollout(scripted_expert, n_episodes=50_000)
policy_net = train_supervised(obs, actions, loss=cross_entropy,
                              mask=invalid_action_mask(obs))
# then RL as usual, from a warm start
ppo_self_play(policy_net, ...)

Note the mask: train the cloned policy only on legal actions, so it inherits the action-masking discipline from Lesson 2.

DAGGER: closing the distribution gap

BC's fatal flaw is distribution shift: the expert's data covers states the expert visits, but your clone — slightly worse — drifts into states the expert never visited, and there it has no training signal, so it makes worse mistakes, drifts further… DAGGER fixes this by iteration: run the current learner, but ask the expert what it would do in the states the learner actually visits, label those, retrain. The dataset grows where the learner actually lives.

repeat:
    states = play_episodes(current_learner)
    labels = scripted_expert.query(states)   # relabel with the expert
    dataset.add(states, labels)
    learner.train(dataset)

The BC ceiling

Cloning converges to the expert's level (a bit below, with shift). A scripted bot has a hard ceiling; the whole point of RL is to break through it. So treat the clone as initialization, not destination: clone, then hand off to PPO or AlphaZero-style self-play. A useful pattern is mixing expert data into early RL batches (like pretraining plus fine-tuning), then annealing it away.

Keystone Imitation buys the first 80% of skill for 1% of the compute; RL exists to earn the last 20% past the teacher.Clone the scripted bots as a warm start, then let self-play take over — never leave the policy at the BC ceiling.
Anti-pattern Treating a cloned policy as a trained agent.You submit it, it loses to the very bot it was cloned from plus epsilon. And if you keep training on expert data only, the policy plateaus at the expert minus distribution-shift losses — the ceiling is structural, not a bug to tune away.

Retrieve it

Why does behavioral cloning reach below-expert performance, even with lots of data?

Distribution shift compounds.

The expert's data covers expert-visited states. The clone errs slightly, lands in unvisited states, has no labels there, errs more — the drift compounds. DAGGER relabels learner-visited states to close exactly this gap.

What does DAGGER change about the training data?

States from learner, labels from expert.

Episodes are played by the current learner, but each visited state is labeled with the expert's action. The dataset therefore covers the states the policy will actually face.

What is the correct handoff from cloning to RL?

Warm start, then anneal.

Initialize the policy net from BC weights, optionally mix expert batches into early PPO updates, and fade the imitation loss to zero. The clone is initialization, not a permanent teacher.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — one evening Take Connect X's "negamax" built-in agent (see kaggle-environments). Run 10k episodes against itself (and vs random), record (board, action), and train a small policy net with cross-entropy over the 7 masked actions. Play clone vs negamax over 200 games. Expect below 50% — then read a Lux AI winner write-up and check how they handled the same ceiling.
