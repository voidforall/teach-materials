The AlphaZero Recipe

RL alone learns slowly because it must guess its way through moves it never tries. AlphaZero combines a policy/value network with tree search: the net guides the search, and the search improves the net's targets. Each half fixes the other's blindness.

The lineage: AlphaGo Zero (Silver et al., 2017) learned Go from pure self-play, then AlphaZero (2018) generalized the recipe to chess and shogi. The mechanism is Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) where a neural net replaces rollouts:

  • Selection — descend the tree picking children by PUCT: Q(s,a) + c·P(s,a)·√Σvisits / (1+N(s,a)). It balances the net's prior P, the average value Q, and visit counts — under-visited moves stay cheap to try.
  • Expansion & evaluation — at a leaf, ask the net once: get priors over moves and a value estimate v(s). No playout to the end.
  • Backup — propagate v(s) (sign-flipped for the opponent in zero-sum play) up the visited path.
play position s → run N sims of MCTS → visit counts π(s)
train net on:  (s, π(s), z)   # z = game outcome from s's perspective
act in the real game:  argmax over π(s)  (or sample with temperature)

The training targets are the key idea: the policy target is not the net's own guess but the MCTS-improved visit distribution, and the value target is the real game outcome. So the net imitates a slightly stronger version of itself, iterated thousands of times — policy improvement and evaluation in one loop. This is why it beats plain PPO in most modern sim competitions: search supplies lookahead the raw policy gradient doesn't have.

The Kaggle reality

Nobody at Kaggle scale reimplements DeepMind's pipeline. The ancestor of nearly every public solution is Junxiao Song's AlphaZero_General: a ~300-line generic loop (MCTS, coach, arena) you specialize with your own game class and network — exactly the skeleton you'd wrap around a kaggle-environments sim. The costs to plan for: self-play games are expensive (every move needs hundreds of net calls), so training is measured in GPU-days and throughput engineering (fast simulator, batched inference, caching) matters as much as the algorithm; and inference at submit time must fit the per-turn time limit, so cap simulations per move and use small nets.

Keystone AlphaZero = MCTS for policy improvement + a policy/value net for guidance, trained on the search's visit counts. The net makes search cheap; the search makes the net's targets stronger than the net itself. That closing loop, not any single component, is the recipe.
Anti-pattern Training the policy on its own raw outputs (a self-distilling loop). If your policy loss target is π_θ instead of the MCTS visit distribution, there is no improvement operator — you're compounding your own bias with extra compute. Related failure: too few simulations per move during self-play; the visit-count targets are then near-random noise and the net learns nothing from them.

Retrieve it

In AlphaZero's MCTS, what replaces the random playouts of classic MCTS?

A single net evaluation.

At each leaf the network returns move priors and a value estimate v(s), which is backed up the path. This is far cheaper than simulating to the terminal state and is where the network's learned intuition enters the search.

What exactly is the policy network's training target for a position?

The MCTS visit counts.

After the simulations, the distribution of visits over moves encodes the search's improved opinion of the position. Training π_θ on it makes the net imitate search-augmented play — the improvement step that compounds over training iterations.

What does the PUCT term's √Σvisits / (1+N(s,a)) factor accomplish?

It forces exploration.

As a sibling's visit count grows, every under-visited action's bonus grows too, so no move stays cheap to try forever. Without it, the prior P(s,a) would lock the search into its first good-looking move and never correct a wrong prior.
