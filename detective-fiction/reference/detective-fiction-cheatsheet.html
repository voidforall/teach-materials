3 · Fair-play quick checklist (Knox / Van Dine, distilled)[4]

Keystone Fair play means: the reader sees every clue the detective sees, before the solution is revealed.
  • The criminal appears early, and is a character of consequence — not a servant, a twin sprung at the end, or a chance passer-by.
  • No secret passages, undiscovered poisons, or last-minute science the reader couldn't know.
  • The detective doesn't solve by unshared intuition; the tale of the pursuit is the tale of the crime.
  • The narrator doesn't lie (the author may conceal, never falsify what is shown).
  • The detective is never the culprit — the game is crooked otherwise.
  • There must be a murder, and it must matter; love interest stays in the background.

5 · Mini-glossary

  • red herring — a clue planted to mislead; fair play requires it to deceive by interpretation, not by omission.
  • fair play — the convention that the attentive reader had every chance to solve it.
  • honkaku — "orthodox" Japanese puzzle mystery; shin-honkaku is its 1987 revival.
  • had-I-but-known — Victorian/Golden Age narration that flags coming disaster without revealing it.
  • cozy — gentle amateur sleuthing, offstage violence, community setting.
  • noir vs hard-boiled — hard-boiled is the tough style; noir is the doomed worldview (protagonist trapped, doomed, often the author of the doom) — the terms overlap but are not synonyms.
  • locked room — an impossible-crime situation whose physical paradox must be solved.
  • MacGuffin — the object everyone wants; its nature matters less than the chase it drives.
  • unreliable narrator — a teller whose account can't be fully trusted; the fairest examples re-read honestly.
  • whodunit / howdunit / whydunit — the identity, the method, or the motive as the central question.
