The Three Life Tasks

Work, friendship, love. Adler says a life is measured by how directly it faces these three — and how much distance it keeps is a diagnosis.

Adler compresses all of living into three life tasks (Lebensaufgaben): work, friendship, and love. The Courage to Be Disliked adds the frame that makes the compression useful: "all problems are interpersonal relationship problems" — and each task is simply a relationship at a different distance. There is no fourth, private task: even the hermit's loneliness is a relation to the human whole, held at maximum distance.

Work: contribution through occupation

The work task is cooperating with society by producing something it needs. Notice how differently that lands from a status reading of career. In Adler's framing, the question "which job suits me?" is downstream of "what does the world need that I can supply?" — the attitude of Lesson 4 in economic form. And the difficulty of the work task, for most people, is not skill but the interpersonal exposure it involves: colleagues, clients, judgment.

Friendship: community by choice

The friendship task is the widest and least scripted: no contract, no blood, no org chart holds it up. Friendship is trust that is freely given and freely renewed — which is why, Adler notes, people who keep only "useful" contacts, or who wait to be approached, are avoiding the task rather than failing at it. The entry move is unilateral: to have a friend is to act as one first.

Love: the most intimate cooperation

The love task — partner and family — Adler describes as the most intimate and the most demanding form of cooperation: "two people moving as if one," each caring for the other more than conspicuously for self. Love, in Adler (against the romantic myth), is not a lightning-strike that happens to you but a task you perform daily — a decision renewed in conduct. "Falling out of love" often names, in Adlerian translation, "I stopped doing the task."

Distance is the diagnosis

Now the diagnostic use. Adler holds that how much distance you keep from each task reveals your courage. The avoidance repertoire is familiar: from work ("I'd start something of my own, but the economy…"), from friendship ("I'm just not a group person"), from love ("I haven't met the right one"). Adler's teacher Rudolf Dreikurs catalogued such "escape patterns" — excuses are not causes of a smaller life but equipment for it (teleology, again, Lesson 1). Every "I can't, because" guarding a task is the felt inferiority of Lesson 2 doing its second job as an excuse.

The answer, in a word Adler made central, is courage (Mut) — and courage here is not fearlessness. Adler's definition is closer to: advancing toward the task while still feeling the disadvantage of doing so. The anxious employee speaks up anyway; the lonely friend makes the call anyway. Courage is a practice with a direction, not a personality trait you wait to acquire.

KeystoneFace all three tasks directly; the distance you keep is the symptom. Work, friendship, and love are three distances of cooperation. Excuses guarding them are equipment for avoidance; courage — advancing with the fear intact — is the only tool that closes distance.
Anti-patternPostponing life until conditions are perfect."Once I'm ready / promoted / healed, then I'll build friendships, love, create." Adler: conditions are never granted, only entered. The perfect condition is a story that keeps the current distance comfortable — a lifestyle choice wearing a calendar.

Retrieve it

Name Adler's three life tasks in one breath.

Work, friendship, and love

Three distances of the same thing — interpersonal cooperation: with society through occupation, with chosen companions, with an intimate partner.

What does the distance you keep from a task reveal?

Your courage, or its shortage

For Adler avoidance is not caused by inability; excuses are equipment for keeping distance, so the distance itself is the diagnostic.

How does Adler define courage?

Advancing while feeling disadvantage

Not fearlessness: courage is moving toward the life task while fully feeling the discomfort — a practice with a direction, not a trait.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 15 minutes Draw three columns: Work / Friendship / Love. Rate your current distance to each: direct, polite, or far. For each "far" or "polite," write the sentence you use to keep the distance ("too busy," "not the right time") and restate it teleologically: "Keeping this distance serves my goal of ___." Choose one task and take one courage-step this week — the call, the registration, the honest sentence. Small and real beats large and planned.
