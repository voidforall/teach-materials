Lifestyle: The Chosen Self

Adler's word for personality is "lifestyle" — a strategy you composed as a child, and can therefore, as an adult, recompose.

Adler's term is Lebensstil — the style of life. By it he means something more systematic than "how you live": the lifestyle is each person's self-consistent grammar — a private way of seeing the world, of answering "who am I, what are others, and what must I do to belong?" Every trait, habit, and symptom is a sentence in this grammar. The pessimist, the charmer, the pleaser, the fighter: each is not a collection of quirks but one coherent strategy for securing a place.

Composed in childhood — by a composer

The lifestyle crystallizes remarkably early — Adler says roughly by age five — out of the child's situation: its position among siblings (the birth order tendencies Adler made famous, best read as flavor, not destiny), the family atmosphere, early memories that the child selects as evidence for its thesis. But the crucial Adlerian point is the one Freud denied: the child is not shaped by these materials like clay. Adler's phrase is the creative self — the child is a painter, not a painting. Given the same household, one child draws "I must be strong," another "I must be charming," another "I must be careful." The materials are given; the composition is chosen.

This is why Adler insisted that early memories are not causes but clues — a person's first remembered scene reveals the lifestyle's thesis already at work, the way an opening line reveals a novel's voice.

"You chose your unhappiness"

The Courage to Be Disliked compresses this into its most confrontational sentence: people choose their lifestyle — and therefore their unhappiness. Not consciously, not maliciously, and not once-for-all: the anxious student of Lesson 2 chose a strategy that once paid off (staying small avoided risk) and keeps renewing it every day, because the familiar unhappiness feels safer than the unfamiliar gamble of change. This is why "it's just my personality" is, in Adlerian ears, a decision announced as a fact.

What is chosen can be re-chosen

And here is the practical payoff that makes Adler a philosopher of attitude rather than a taxonomist of types. If the lifestyle were fixed temperament, therapy would be management. Because it is a strategy, Adlerian therapy is re-education: surfacing the grammar, showing its costs, and continuing life with a new one. Adler reports cases of profound change at every age; the door never rusts shut. What keeps a lifestyle in place is not rigidity of brain but the courage it takes to abandon a known strategy for an unknown one — which is why, as we will see in Lesson 5, Adler's key virtue is not insight but courage.

KeystoneYou are the author of your story, not its transcript. "Personality" is a strategy composed long ago for a child's purposes; what was composed can be recomposed. The sentence "that's just who I am" marks the exact spot where authorship was set down.
Anti-patternTreating personality as hardware."I'm an introvert / an anxious type / not a math person" — each is software presenting itself as firmware. The claim forecloses the very experiments (speaking up, starting, trying) that could rewrite the strategy, and it does so precisely to avoid their risk.

Retrieve it

What does "lifestyle" (Lebensstil) mean in Adler?

One coherent personal life strategy

Not habits or taste, but the private grammar answering who I am, who others are, and what I must do to belong — the single thesis behind traits and symptoms.

What does "creative self" say about childhood?

The child composes, not merely receives

Materials (family, birth order, health) are given, but the child paints a strategy from them; identical households yield different lifestyles.

Why does a lifestyle persist when it makes us unhappy?

Familiar unhappiness feels safer than change

The old strategy is known and once paid off; abandoning it for an unfamiliar one takes courage — the resource Adler's therapy works to supply.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 15 minutes Write down your earliest memory you can recall, in detail. Then, as Adler would, read it not as history but as thesis: what view of self, others, and "what I must do" does this little scene still encode? ("I had to be the careful one." "Attention must be seized.") Then write one sentence of a different strategy you could compose today. You have just read your own lifestyle's opening line — and drafted a revision.
