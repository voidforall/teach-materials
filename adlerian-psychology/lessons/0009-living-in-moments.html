Living in Moments: Happiness as Contribution

The last idea before the capstone: life is not a line to a summit but a series of moments — a dance. Happiness is not at the destination; it is the feeling of contributing, now.

Most people unconsciously draw life as a trajectory: a line from a starting point to a grand destination — the degree, the company, the recognition, the summit — with "real life" beginning at arrival. The Courage to Be Disliked replaces the drawing: life is dynamism, a series of moments, each complete in itself. Its image is the dance: when you dance, you do not pass through the dance to reach a point on the floor — the dancing is the thing. "Live each moment earnestly," the philosopher says; "it is quite possible to regard the moments when one dances as the journey."

Goals as direction, not deferral

This does not abolish goals. It demotes them from destinations to direction — back to Adler's guiding star (Lesson 8): you steer by a star you will never touch. The pathological version is deferred living: "once I make partner / finish the degree / find the one, my life begins." Teleology (Lesson 1) reads this coldly: the deferral is doing a job — keeping today's risks unattempted, today's tasks at a comfortable distance (Lesson 5). People on their deathbeds, Adler notes, are not sorry about goals unreached; they grieve moments unlived.

Happiness, defined

With ordinary worth secured (Lesson 8) and contribution in play (Lesson 4), the course can state the Adlerian definition plainly: happiness is the feeling of contribution (貢献感, in the book's Japanese) — the felt sense, right now, of being of use to someone, without needing a certificate for it. That last clause is the hard one. If you must be told you contributed — thanked, promoted, applauded — the feeling collapses whenever the audience looks away, and you are back to recognition-seeking (Lesson 6). The three acceptances of Lesson 8 are precisely what lets the feeling stand on its own: I accept myself, I trust others, therefore my sense of being useful can rest in my own task, done.

Meaning is given, not found

The course's final theoretical move. Asked "what is the meaning of life?", the Courage philosopher answers: there is no general meaning of life — whatever meaning each of us gives to life, that is the meaning of life. Adler himself, an MD who had watched the influenza epidemic take patients wholesale, resisted the idea that any life could be "wasted" by its length or ending: a life's meaning is not a verdict rendered at its close; it is composed moment by moment while lived. On this view, no day is a corridor, and no ordinary Tuesday is rehearsal.

KeystoneLife is a dance, and happiness is the feeling of contributing — now, uncertified. Goals are stars to steer by, not stations where living starts. The meaning of life is not found; it is what you give, moment by moment.
Anti-patternThe corridor life. Treating the present as passage — tolerating days, deferring love and enjoyment to arrival — and demanding certificates of contribution (thanks, applause, metrics) before permitting yourself the feeling of being useful. The corridor has no exit; the certificate office never closes.

Retrieve it

What image replaces life-as-trajectory in Adler?

Dancing — a series of moments

Each moment is complete in itself; you do not dance to reach a spot on the floor, and life's moments are not passages to a summit.

What is happiness, in the Adlerian definition?

The feeling of contributing now

The felt sense of being of use to someone in the present — standing on its own, not requiring thanks or applause as a certificate.

What is the meaning of life, per the course?

Whatever meaning you give it

No general meaning exists to be found; meaning is composed moment by moment while living, not issued as a verdict at the end.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — today Choose one ordinary hour — a commute, a meal, a work block — and treat it as the dance: do it earnestly, as if it were the whole of life, not a corridor to later. At the end, note one concrete way the hour was of use to someone (even just you, well-tended). No certificate required. Notice whether the hour felt different from corridor-hours. That difference is the lesson.
