Separation of Tasks

Adler's sharpest practical tool is one question: whose task is this? Almost all interpersonal agony, it turns out, is trespassing.

Of all the instruments in this course, this is the one you can use before dinner. The Courage to Be Disliked calls it separation of tasks: for any burden, ask "whose task is this?" — and decide with a single test: who ultimately bears the consequences of the outcome? The one who bears the consequences owns the task. Your business ends exactly at that boundary.

The test in action

A child will not study. Who bears the consequences of not studying — in the end, and in their own life? The child. Studying is the child's task. The parent's task is to assist: make study attractive, be available, model curiosity. The moment the parent seizes the task — nagging, bribing, sitting over the homework — the child learns that studying is something done to satisfy a parent, and the parent's "help" becomes the child's resistance. Note what this is not: it is not neglect. Assistance offered without takeover is respect.

You give your honest opinion; your colleague resents it. Stating your view sincerely is your task. How they take it — resentment, gratitude, indifference — is theirs. You cannot do their task for them, and bending your message into manipulation is an attempt to.

You post your work online. Making it as good as you can: your task. The likes, the mockery, the silence: the audience's task, never yours to perform.

Freedom is being disliked

Now the deep layer. Why do we trespass so constantly — taking over others' tasks, and living others' expectations of ours? Because we want recognition (yo-nin, in the book's Japanese): approval, a stamp from someone above us certifying that we are good. Adler's verdict is harsh: recognition-seeking is choosing an unfree lifestyle. If your actions need others' approval to count, then others' expectations become your task list — you are living their life, not yours. The Courage to Be Disliked's most famous line lands here: "Freedom is being disliked by other people." Not that you should seek dislike — but that a life in which no one could ever dislike you is a life lived to others' specifications.

Recognize the pattern's cost in the other direction too: demanding that others live up to your expectations (a partner who "should" be more ambitious, a child who "should" love your field) is trespassing with your expectations onto their task.

Boundaries are the precondition of love

People fear this tool makes relationships cold. Adler's answer is the reverse: separation is what makes real closeness possible. When I stop managing your feelings as my responsibility and stop assigning you mine, what remains between us is two whole people choosing each other — not two mutual overseers. Unconditional trust (Lesson 8's "confidence in others") is only possible across a clean boundary: I trust as my task; whether you honor it is yours.

KeystoneDo your task fully; release everyone else's. The consequences test tells you whose task it is. Across the boundary: assist when asked, state your view sincerely, refuse to make others' verdicts your project. That refusal is what freedom is made of.
Anti-patternThe double trespass.Taking over others' tasks ("I know best how to live your life") and forcing your expectations onto them — while, in mirror image, running your whole life to satisfy others' expectations and calling it duty. Both directions produce the same thing: exhausted overseers, supervised intimates, no one actually living.

Retrieve it

What single test decides whose task something is?

Who ultimately bears the consequences

The bearer of the outcome's consequences owns the task; everyone else's role ends at assistance and sincerity.

Why is recognition-seeking an unfree lifestyle?

It makes others' expectations your task list

If actions need approval to count, you live to others' specifications; Adler ties freedom precisely to tolerating being disliked.

Why isn't task separation coldness?

It enables freely chosen closeness

When neither party manages the other, what remains is two whole people choosing each other — the precondition of real love and trust.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — this week Carry the consequences test for seven days. Each time you feel the urge to intervene in someone's choice (or the ache of their judgment of yours), write one line: the situation, whose task, and what "doing only my task" would look like. At the end of the week, count how much of your daily worry was, by the test, spent on other people's tasks. That number is your available freedom.
