The Third Viennese School: Adler's Break with Freud

Before Adler gives us a life attitude, he takes something away: the alibi of the past. This is the founding move of individual psychology.

Alfred Adler (1870–1937) was a Vienna physician who joined Freud's inner circle in 1902, was president of the psychoanalytic society — and walked out in 1911 after a decade of mounting disagreement. His individual psychology is counted, alongside Freud's psychoanalysis and Jung's analytical psychology, as the third of the three Viennese schools of depth psychology. The split was not a quarrel about details. It was a fork in how to read a human life.

Etiology vs teleology: two ways to read a person

Freud's method is etiological: it looks for causes in the past. A neurosis today is the effect of a trauma then; the person is, in a sense, pushed by history. Adler's method is teleological: it looks for goals in the present. "Telos" means goal — and Adler's claim is that our emotions, symptoms, and habits are not forces acting on us but instruments we deploy for something. The man who cannot leave the house is not chained by his anxiety; his anxiety serves his goal of not leaving the house.

Adler's most quoted formulation is stark: "We are not determined by our experiences, but by the meaning we give them." Two children grow up with the same harsh father; one concludes people are dangerous, the other concludes he must become protective. The experience did not decide anything — the interpretation, chosen for the child's own purposes, did. On this see Adler's own writing and the lucid popularization by Kishimi & Koga, The Courage to Be Disliked, from which the course borrows several worked examples.

The red face that served a purpose

Kishimi & Koga's philosopher offers the classic case. A young man says: "I have social anxiety. I blush whenever I must speak to people, so I cannot make friends — my symptom ruins my life." A Freudian would excavate the past for the trauma that planted the blushing. Adler reverses the arrow: the young man's goal is to avoid the risk of rejection in relationships, and the blushing is manufactured — selected, maintained — as the perfect excuse. Proof by counter-example: people with the same blush respond differently when the excuse is removed; and the "cure" that Adler predicts is not of the skin but of the goal. Change the goal and the symptom loses its job.

This is why Adler bristles at the word "trauma" as a cause. Nothing that happens to you decides your life. Events supply material; you supply the meaning and the direction. That is not a denial that suffering is real — it is a claim about where the steering wheel sits.

Why this is good news

Notice what the teleological view buys you. If the past caused you, you are a patient waiting for excavation. If present goals steer you, you are an agent who can re-choose the destination — today, without anyone's permission. Adler, the socialist-born doctor who ran free child-guidance clinics in Vienna's working-class districts, built his whole psychology on this democratic optimism: people are not broken machines but goal-directed wholes ("individual" in individual psychology means indivisible), equally capable of change.

KeystoneYou are pulled by goals, not pushed by the past. Every persistent emotion and symptom is doing a job — protecting you from some risk, excusing you from some task. Ask of any feeling: "What is this for?" not "What caused this?" The first question opens a door; the second only digs a hole.
Anti-patternUsing your biography as a permit. "I can't form relationships because of my childhood"; "I procrastinate because of how I was raised." Adler would say: the childhood is real, the pain was real — but the sentence is a present-tense choice, protecting you from the risk of trying. History explains your materials; it does not sign your name.

Retrieve it

What single word names Adler's method, and against what did it define itself?

Teleology — against Freudian etiology

Etiology hunts causes in the past; teleology reads present goals. Adler holds that symptoms and emotions are instruments serving aims, not scars of history.

Adler says experiences don't determine us. What does determine us?

The meaning we give them

Identical events yield opposite lives depending on the interpretation adopted — and interpretations are chosen for purposes, so they can be re-chosen.

In the blushing example, what does the symptom actually serve?

The goal of avoiding rejection

The anxiety is manufactured as an excuse that legitimizes withdrawal from the risky task of relating to others; remove the goal and the symptom loses function.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 10 minutes Write down one sentence about yourself of the form "I can't ___ because ___ (past fact)." Now rewrite it teleologically: "Not doing ___ serves my goal of ___." What risk, exactly, is the not-doing protecting you from? Name it in writing. You have just performed the first move of Adlerian therapy on yourself — no couch required.

Why this matters for the mission

The mission asks what life attitude Adler prescribes. The attitude begins here: stop archiving your past as evidence and start auditing your present goals. Every later lesson — inferiority as fuel (Lesson 2), the re-choosable lifestyle (Lesson 3), task separation and courage — is downstream of this single reversal of the causal arrow.
