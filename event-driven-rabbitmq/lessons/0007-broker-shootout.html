Task Queue or Log of Record?

Choosing a broker is one question, not a feature matrix — and the answer is often "both."

The comparison you actually need isn't a checklist of features. Three tools, three different answers to the question "what happens to a message after it's handled?" — and everything else follows from that design commitment.1

Keystone — the decision procedure Ask in order: (1) Work or history? Work to be done once and deleted → RabbitMQ or SQS. History to re-read → Kafka. (2) If work: routing or ops? Rich routing, retry ladders, multi-protocol, on your infra → RabbitMQ. No infra team, already on AWS, simple queues → SQS (+SNS for fan-out). (3) If history: you're done — Kafka, partitioned by entity for ordering (L5's consistent-hash logic, native). And the mature answer at scale is routinely both: Kafka as the stream of record, RabbitMQ as the work queues downstream of it.

Why the hybrids fail and succeed

Two classic misfits Kafka as a task queue: the log has no per-message ack — a failed job can't be "returned"; offsets move for the whole partition, poison handling (L4) is DIY, and skipping ahead to unblock means acknowledging failures. It works, grudgingly, for idempotent bulk pipelines — it hurts for per-item work.

RabbitMQ as an event store: acked messages are gone; consumers that fall behind and reconnect can't replay yesterday's stream; "keep the messages" means never acking — which caps throughput and re-creates the unacked flood from Lesson 2. Retention is fundamentally not what queues do.

When the hybrid succeeds: Kafka holds the immutable orders stream every team replays at will; one consumer's job is thin — tail the log and publish work items onto RabbitMQ exchanges, where routing, retries, and parking lots (all of L1–L5) do their thing. Each tool holds its half of the at-least-once contract with idempotency at the seam.

What your RabbitMQ course knowledge maps to

  • Acks (L2): Kafka's equivalent is the committed offset — per consumer group, not per message. SQS has DeleteMessage after a visibility timeout: forget to delete fast and the message reappears — at-least-once with extra steps, same idempotency bill (L3).
  • Competing consumers (L1): Kafka consumer groups give it natively — partition-count is your parallelism ceiling. SQS gives it free; that's its best trick.
  • Ordering (L5): Kafka is strict per partition — you get the consistent-hash design whether you asked or not; a hot entity still hot-dogs one partition. RabbitMQ's best-effort needs the guard patterns either way.
  • Retry ladders (L4): pure RabbitMQ idiom. Kafka needs application-level retry topics; SQS has redrive policies — a built-in parking lot, actually its nicest opinion.

Check yourself

Q1. Finance needs to reprocess every order event after a bug. Which broker?

Kafka — retained log, rewind consumers to any offset.

Replay is the defining capability of a log of record. In RabbitMQ, acked history is gone; retention-less queues can't retrofit it.

Q2. A five-person startup on AWS needs a job queue. Which broker?

SQS — zero operations, pay per request, no cluster.

No routing finesse needed, no infra team to run RabbitMQ. Revisit when routing complexity or on-prem needs arrive (SNS covers broadcast).

Q3. Kafka consumer fails one record in a partition. What's missing?

A per-message ack — offsets move for everything.

The whole partition's position commits; the failed record needs application-level retry topics or it blocks/skips with the batch.

Q4. Why does SQS redeliver messages you haven't deleted yet?

Visibility timeout expiry returns the message to service.

It's at-least-once delivery (L2/L3) wearing different clothes: same redeliveries, same idempotency requirement.

Now you drive — three calls to make

Argue each choice — before you reveal

(a) A rideshare company: driver-location stream (10k events/s) feeding ETA models and a live map, plus dispatch jobs ("match this ride") that must run once. (b) A bank: payment-events archive, every downstream audit consumer replays independently, new consumers join quarterly. (c) Your own work system — whatever you're building now. For each: broker, why, and the one risk your choice imports.

Reveal the reasoning

(a) Both. The location stream is history-shaped — Kafka, partitioned by ride/driver ID; replayable map backfills and model training are exactly log strengths. Dispatch is work — RabbitMQ: exactly-one matching matters more than replay, retries and parking lots are idiomatic. Risk: two brokers to operate, idempotent seam between them.

(b) Kafka, clearly — the requirements are a definition of a log of record: retention, independent consumer groups, replay for newcomers. Key by account ID for per-account ordering. Risk: deletion/GDPR needs log compaction design, and partition count is set early-ish (adding partitions reorders keys' placement).

(c) The honest answer uses the keystone's procedure: work or history first, routing-vs-ops second, and it's fine for the first version to be just RabbitMQ — the whole L1–L6 toolkit applies — with Kafka added when a replay consumer actually appears, not before.

The skill being drilled is refusing feature-matrix thinking: each requirement above names its broker by the after-it's-handled question, not by throughput numbers or fashion.
