The Message Isn't Gone Until You Ack It

Delivery semantics from the consumer's side: acks, redelivery, and the prefetch window.

Last lesson you learned where messages go. This one is about when a message stops existing — and it's the difference between a system that survives a crash and one that quietly loses work. Everything here is one idea with consequences.1

The delivery contract

When your consumer receives a message, the broker puts it in a limbo state for your channel: unacked ("delivered, awaiting verdict"). The message is hidden from other consumers but not deleted. You then return exactly one of three verdicts:

The three verdicts a consumer can return
CallMeaningBroker's response
ackhandled, donedelete the message
nack / rejectcan't handle itrequeue it, or dead-letter it (Lesson 4)
(connection dies)no verdict arrivedrequeue automatically
Keystone A message is deleted only on ack. Crash, timeout, channel closed — anything short of an ack means the broker requeues and redelivers, to you or a competitor. That's at-least-once delivery: you may see the same message twice, never zero times. Every consumer you write must be safe to run twice on the same message — idempotent. This is not a caveat; it's the contract you design against.

The failure scenario, played out

Consumer A crashes mid-m1 with no ack → broker requeues m1 → competitor B gets it. Work queue semantics from Lesson 1 + at-least-once: B may be re-doing work A already half-finished.

Auto-ack: the footgun

Anti-pattern auto-ack (no-ack mode) tells the broker to delete messages as it sends them. Crash mid-processing and that work is gone forever — at-most-once delivery. Legit only for lossy work (metrics, live ticks). For anything your business cares about: manual acks, and ack after processing, not on receipt.

The matching beginner bug is the inverse: ack-on-receipt, then process. That's auto-ack with extra steps — the crash window just moved. Placement of the ack call is your durability policy.

Prefetch: the flow-control dial

Without limits, the broker floods a consumer with thousands of unacked messages — all stuck in limbo, none processed, and if the consumer dies, all of them requeue in a thundering herd. prefetch (QoS) caps the count of unacked messages per consumer channel.2

  • prefetch = 0 (unlimited): the flood above. Never in production.
  • prefetch = 1: one at a time — fair, slow. Right when processing is expensive or strictly order-sensitive within a consumer.
  • prefetch = 10–100: the usual sweet spot. The consumer always has the next message locally; throughput rises sharply from 1, flattens after a few dozen.

Prefetch is also your fairness mechanism: with competing consumers, the broker only dispatches to a consumer that has window space — a slow consumer stops hogging messages instead of hoarding them.

Check yourself

Q1. Your consumer processed a message, then crashed before acking. Redelivered?

Yes — no ack means the broker requeues the message.

Hence at-least-once, hence idempotent handlers: this exact sequence is routine, not exceptional.

Q2. Where exactly in your handler does the ack call belong?

After processing fully succeeds — the very last step.

Ack placement is the durability policy. Ack-on-receipt equals auto-ack with extra steps.

Q3. Prefetch is 1,000 and a consumer dies holding 900 unacked messages. Effect?

All 900 requeue at once — a thundering herd.

Huge windows also starve competitors, since one consumer can hoard the entire queue. Keep it modest.

Q4. A redelivered message carries which marker, and what do you do with it?

The redelivered flag is set on it.

Use it as a signal — check it in dead-letter/retry logic (Lesson 4), and never as a substitute for idempotency: the flag can be lost across topology changes.

Now you drive

Audit your code — before you reveal

Find (or imagine) one consumer you've written at work and answer: (a) auto-ack or manual? (b) if manual, where is the ack — receipt or completion? (c) is the handler idempotent — what happens if the same message runs twice? (d) what prefetch, and was it chosen or defaulted?

What a passing audit looks like

(a) manual acks; (b) ack after the handler's success path completes — including DB commit; (c) duplicate-safe, typically by deduplication key or upsert (same row written twice = no-op); (d) an explicit, modest value (10–100), tuned if throughput matters.

Most real-world consumers fail (b) or (c). If yours fails (c), that's not a RabbitMQ problem — it's a handler design problem, and Lesson 3 doubles down on why.
