Capstone: Value a Company

One company, one story, one spreadsheet — and an honest confrontation with your own biases. This is where the nine previous lessons become a skill.

Take the company you wrote three sentences about in Lesson 1. Build the full model. Sequence matters — story first, then numbers, then value, then interrogation:

  1. Story — where is this firm on the life cycle (Lesson 7)? Young / mature / declining? Write the narrative in two sentences.
  2. Cash flows — current revenue, margins, ROC, reinvestment rate from filings (Lesson 4). Normalize for one-time items; capitalize R&D if material.
  3. Discount rate — risk-free + β×ERP with a bottom-up beta, blended into a WACC that drifts down as the firm matures (Lessons 5–6).
  4. Growth path — start at fundamental growth g = reinvestment × ROC, fade toward GDP over 5–10 years, with reinvestment falling alongside (Lesson 7).
  5. Terminal value — stable-growth perpetuity with terminal reinvestment = g/ROC, mature WACC, fading excess returns (Lesson 8).
  6. Value per share — sum, subtract net debt, divide by shares.
  7. Cross-check — what does the market's current price imply? Reverse the DCF (solve for the g or margin the price needs) and compare with your story (Lesson 9's multiples as a second opinion).

The bias audit

Damodaran's real capstone lesson (the closing sessions of every class, and Reflections on Valuation): the model is never the problem — you are. The three standard corruptions:

  • Anchoring — your value drifts toward the market price because you saw it first. Antidote: write your story and key assumptions before checking the price.
  • Confirmation — you already like/hate the company, and the assumptions quietly obey. Antidote: revalue assuming the opposite thesis is true; see which changes feel unbearable.
  • Certainty — a point estimate hides the uncertainty. Antidote: present a distribution — run your model at your pessimistic/base/optimistic story and own the range.
Keystone A valuation is an honesty device, not a price machine. Its product is not "the number" but a clarified, quantified story plus a list of what you're really betting on. If you can't name your bet, you haven't valued the company.
Anti-pattern Defending the model. The moment your response to a challenge is spreadsheet precision rather than a revised story, you've stopped valuing and started lawyering. Damodaran's standard: change one assumption at a time, note how value moves, and let the sensitivity — not your ego — decide what matters.

Retrieve it

Name the seven-step sequence from story to value.

Story, cash flows, discount, growth, terminal, per-share, cross-check.

Order matters: story disciplines inputs before any arithmetic can seduce you, and the market-implied cross-check closes the loop without ever anchoring the inputs.

Price is 30% above your value. What's the honest output?

A bet, clearly stated.

Either the market's story differs from yours (identify exactly which input), or you're missing something. Output = "I'm betting growth/margins/risk equals X; if the market's Y is right, I'm wrong" — not "the market is irrational."

Which single check catches the most broken DCFs?

Recompute g from reinvestment.

g = reinvestment × ROC (Lesson 7) fails in most amateur models — growth assumed without the reinvestment to pay for it, or terminal perpetuities violating GDP caps. It's the fastest full-body scan.

Deliverables: (1) the story paragraph; (2) the model; (3) three-scenario value range; (4) the market-implied story at today's price; (5) one paragraph: "my bet is on ___". Then read one Damodaran blog valuation of a household name (Musings on Markets) and compare his honesty moves to yours.
