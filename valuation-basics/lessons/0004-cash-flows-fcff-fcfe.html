Estimating Cash Flows: FCFF & FCFE

Accounting earnings are an opinion; cash is a fact. Free cash flow is the cash a business generates that could be paid out without harming future growth — and it is always a subtraction story.

Two definitions, from Investment Valuation. Start from after-tax operating income and subtract what growth consumes:

FCFF = EBIT × (1 − tax rate)
     − (Capital expenditures − Depreciation)
     − Δ Working capital

Each line has a job. EBIT(1−t) is the cash operating earnings of the business, blind to how it's financed. Net capex replaces what wore out and adds productive capacity. Working capital changes capture the cash quietly absorbed by inventory and receivables when you grow. What survives is genuinely free.

For the equity version, put financing back in:

FCFE = Net income
     + Depreciation
     − Capital expenditures
     − Δ Working capital
     − (New debt issued − Debt repaid)

Reading the statement like a valuer

Estimating these lines from real filings is mostly reclassification, not arithmetic:

  • Strip out one-time items (restructuring, lawsuit settlements) — Damodaran normalizes earnings to what the business would have earned routinely.
  • Capitalize leases — treat lease payments as debt; otherwise retailers look asset-light and under-levered.
  • R&D and (for some) SG&A are investments, not expenses — Damodaran's signature adjustment: amortize R&D like an asset, since it builds future cash flows. This single change can transform the value of tech and pharma firms.
Keystone Free cash flow = earnings minus reinvestment. Growth is not free; every dollar of it must be financed by capex and working capital. A company can report rising earnings while its free cash flow collapses — that divergence is the smell test.
Anti-pattern Worshiping negative FCF because "Amazon." Low FCF from heavy reinvestment at high returns is a growth story; low FCF from shrinking margins and bloated working capital is decay. The number alone tells you nothing — the reason for the subtraction does. (Amazon is exactly Damodaran's canonical example of getting this distinction right.)

Retrieve it

EBIT $200, tax 25%, capex $80, depreciation $50, ΔWC $20. FCFF?

$150 − $30 − $20 = $100.

After-tax EBIT = 200×0.75 = 150; net capex = 80−50 = 30; plus working capital absorption 20. The firm generated $100 of cash available to all claimholders.

Why add depreciation back but subtract capex?

Depreciation isn't cash.

Depreciation reduced EBIT without spending cash, so it returns; capex is real cash leaving. Only the net (capex − depreciation) is the true reinvestment above maintenance.

Why does Damodaran capitalize R&D?

R&D builds future cash flows.

Expensing a multi-year asset in one year understates current earnings and assets — systematically penalizing R&D-heavy firms. Treating R&D as capex (amortized over the asset life) puts R&D-intensive companies on comparable footing.

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 25 minutes Pull the latest annual report of a company you know. Compute one year of FCFF: EBIT × (1−effective tax rate) − net capex − ΔWC (use cash-flow-statement lines: capex, depreciation, and change in inventory+receivables−payables). Compare it to reported net income. Which is bigger, and what does the gap tell you about the story? Ask the agent to sanity-check your numbers.
