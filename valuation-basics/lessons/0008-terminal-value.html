Terminal Value

Typically 60–80% of a DCF's value sits in the last line: the value of all cash flows after your forecast ends. Terrifying — unless you make that line obey the same economics as everything else.

Two routes to the terminal value, from Session 12:

1. Stable-growth (forever) model:
   TV_n = FCFF_{n+1} / (WACC − g),   g ≤ risk-free rate ≈ nominal GDP

2. Exit multiple:
   TV_n = EV/EBITDA_n × EBITDA_n     (a pricing device smuggled into a DCF)

Damodaran's verdict: the multiple route is fine if you're honest that you've stopped doing intrinsic valuation — the exit multiple imports the market's pricing of peers into your "intrinsic" value. If you use it, it must be consistent with your stable-growth assumptions; a useful cross-check is computing both and reconciling.

What "stable" means

The terminal year isn't just the forecast's last year extended forever — the firm must arrive at stable-company properties:

  • Growth at or below the risk-free rate.
  • Beta drifts toward 1; debt ratio toward a mature target (often 20–25%); WACC falls.
  • Reinvestment matches growth: in the terminal year, reinvestment = g/ROC. The classic error is assuming high terminal growth with no reinvestment — value created from nothing.
  • Excess returns decay: forever-high ROC above cost of capital is a moat claim. Damodaran's default lets excess returns fade toward zero in the terminal year for competitive industries, persisting only for genuine, durable moats.
TV = FCFF_n × (1+g) / (WACC_terminal − g)
where FCFF_terminal already embeds terminal reinvestment = g / ROC_terminal
Keystone The terminal value is a DCF of its own. It must obey g = reinvestment × ROC, a mature WACC, and growth capped by GDP. Get the terminal assumptions internally consistent and the big number stops being scary; leave them inconsistent and it's a wish.
Anti-pattern Perpetual excess returns. Assuming your company earns 25% on capital forever with a 8% WACC. Competition, capital mobility, and history all say otherwise — Damodaran's rule of thumb: excess returns last a decade or two, not eternity. Pick a fade schedule and defend it.

Retrieve it

Terminal FCFF $200, g 2.5%, WACC 7.5%. TV at year n?

200 × 1.025 / 0.05 = $4,100.

Growing perpetuity on next year's flow, discounted to today separately by (1+WACC)^n. Note how a 0.5% change in WACC or g swings TV by ~10% — terminal sensitivity is the sensitivity.

Terminal g is 3%, ROC_terminal is 10%. Required terminal reinvestment?

3/10 = 30% of after-tax EBIT.

Reinvestment = g/ROC. The remaining 70% becomes the FCFF inside the perpetuity — skip this subtraction and you've double-counted growth.

Your DCF's terminal value is 95% of total value. What does that tell you?

The forecast is nearly meaningless.

All the value lives in assumptions you can't observe — either extend/structure the forecast, or admit the valuation is a bet on terminal assumptions (typical for young growth firms, where honesty means saying exactly that).

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 15 minutes Take the mini-DCF from Lesson 2. Recompute the terminal value under three g's (1.5%, 2.5%, 3.5%) with reinvestment adjusted as g/ROC for ROC = 12%. Watch total value move. Write one sentence: "my valuation's real bet is on ___."
