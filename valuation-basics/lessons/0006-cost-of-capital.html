Beta, Debt & WACC

The cost of capital is a weighted average of what each class of capital costs. The art is entirely in beta — and beta is not a statistics exercise, it's a statement about a business.

From Sessions 8–9, the full rate stack for a firm DCF:

Cost of equity = Risk-free rate + β × ERP            (Lesson 5's anchors)
Cost of debt   = Risk-free rate + default spread      (from rating or ratios)
               × (1 − marginal tax rate)              (debt gets a tax shield)

WACC = E/(D+E) × cost of equity + D/(D+E) × after-tax cost of debt

Market values only: equity at current market cap, debt at market value of the debt (book is a common proxy), using the company's target debt ratio, not today's accident.

Beta: three ways, one idea

A regression beta (stock vs index) is noise: short windows, wide standard errors, drift toward whatever you anchored the service to. Damodaran's ladder of fixes:

  1. Bottom-up beta (his default): average the unlevered betas of the business's industries, then relever for the company's own debt:
β_levered = β_unlevered × (1 + (1−t) × D/E)
  • Financial-leverage logic: debt amplifies equity's exposure to business risk — same business, more debt, higher beta.
  • Two structural determinants: what the business sells (cyclical products → high beta) and how (high fixed costs → high operating leverage → high beta). Utilities sell steady demand: β ≈ 0.2–0.4. Luxury goods and semis swing with the cycle: β > 1.2.

    • The beta you choose should be defensible in business language before it's defensible in statistics.

    Keystone Beta measures the business, not the ticker. Bottom-up industry betas, relevered for the firm's actual debt, beat any regression. And WACC is only as honest as its weights: market values, target mix, marginal tax rate.
    Anti-pattern WACC as a constant. As a firm grows and matures, its debt capacity, beta, and mix all change — a young firm's WACC of 12% should glide toward 8% in the terminal years. Using one WACC across the whole model quietly misvalues both ends.

    Retrieve it

    Unlevered beta 1.0, D/E = 0.5, tax 25%. Levered beta?

    1.0 × 1.375 = 1.375.

    β_L = β_U(1 + (1−t)(D/E)) = 1 × (1 + 0.75×0.5). Debt magnifies equity's exposure to the same business risk by a third.

    Why does debt get a tax shield but equity doesn't?

    Interest is tax-deductible.

    Dividends are paid after tax; interest is a pre-tax expense. The government effectively subsidizes part of the interest cost, so the effective cost of debt is r_d(1−t).

    Two identical businesses, one with 60% debt. Higher equity beta where?

    The levered one.

    Same unlevered beta, but D/E relevers it upward — fixed debt payments concentrate the business's swings onto fewer equity dollars. This is also why distressed firms show wild betas.

    Prove it to yourself

    Try it — 15 minutes From Damodaran's current beta dataset, find the unlevered beta of an industry you care about. Relever it at your company's D/E and tax rate; compute cost of equity with Lesson 5's ERP; blend with an after-tax cost of debt (guess the spread from its bond rating, or Baa ≈ 2%). You now hold a complete, defensible WACC.
