Price vs Value

Everything in this course rests on one distinction: the price the market pays is not the value the asset holds. Valuation is the discipline of estimating the second without being hypnotized by the first.

Damodaran opens every one of his valuation classes with this split. An asset has an intrinsic value determined by its fundamentals — the cash flows it will generate, their timing, and their uncertainty. A price is what someone pays, driven by demand, mood, momentum and narrative. The two can diverge for a long time, and that divergence is both the risk and the opportunity.

From this distinction follow the three ways people attach numbers to assets:

  • Intrinsic valuation (DCF) — value from expected cash flows, discounted for time and risk. The core of this course.
  • Relative valuation (pricing) — value from what similar assets cost: multiples like P/E and EV/EBITDA. How most real-world transactions are actually "valued."
  • Contingent claim (option) valuation — treating the asset as an option when payoffs are asymmetric. Out of scope here, but named so you recognize it.

Neither of the first two is "more correct." Damodaran's point is that you must know which one you are doing. A DCF says what the asset is worth given your story about its fundamentals; a multiple says what the market will probably pay given what it pays for peers. Most bad analysis is a multiple wearing a DCF costume.

The bridge: story and numbers

A valuation is useless as a spreadsheet alone. In The Little Book of Valuation, Damodaran insists every valuation is a bridge between a story about the company (young, growing, risky) and the numbers (growth rates, margins, discount rates). If your story can't become numbers, it's a fantasy; if your numbers have no story, they're arbitrary. Good valuers iterate: story → numbers → check the numbers → revise the story.

Keystone Price is set by the market; value is set by cash flows, growth, and risk. Valuation estimates the second. Pricing tracks the first. Know at all times which of the two you are producing — and never let the market's price quietly become your "value."
Anti-pattern Reverse-engineering your target. Starting from the price you want to justify and hunting for growth/margin assumptions that produce it. Damodaran calls this the most common corruption of valuation — the "garbage in, gospel out" trap where a spreadsheet's precision disguises driven assumptions.

Retrieve it

What three inputs determine intrinsic value?

Cash flows, growth, risk.

Every DCF, however elaborate, reduces to how much cash the asset generates, how fast that grows, and how uncertain it is (the discount rate). The whole course builds these three one at a time.

You value a firm by averaging the P/E of five peers. Is that a valuation?

No — it's pricing.

Relative valuation tells you what the market likely pays for comparable assets, not what the asset is inherently worth. It inherits whatever mispricing infects the peer group.

Why does Damodaran demand a "story" alongside the numbers?

Numbers need narrative grounding.

The story disciplines the assumptions (a 25% growth rate means something concrete about the market and moat) and the numbers discipline the story (if your narrative implies impossible margins, the story is wrong).

Prove it to yourself

Try it — 10 minutes, no spreadsheet Pick a company you find interesting. Write three sentences: (1) its story in your words — young or mature? growing or stable? what edge?; (2) what the market's price currently implies about that story (optimistic? pessimistic?); (3) which of the three approaches (DCF, multiples, options) you'd trust for it and why. Keep this note — the capstone will turn it into a full valuation.
