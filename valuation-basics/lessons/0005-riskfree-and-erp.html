The Risk-Free Rate & the Equity Risk Premium

Every discount rate you will ever use is anchored by two numbers: what time is worth (risk-free rate) and what average risk is worth (equity risk premium). Estimate these badly and no cleverness downstream can save the valuation.

The risk-free rate has two requirements Damodaran insists on: no default risk and no reinvestment risk. That means matching the cash flow's currency and horizon. A 10-year government bond in the cash flow's currency is the standard proxy — but a defaultable government (think emerging markets) overstates the "risk-free" rate; his fix is to net out the sovereign default spread (from the country's CDS or rating).

The equity risk premium (ERP) is the extra return investors demand for holding the average-risk equity portfolio instead of the risk-free asset:

Cost of equity = Risk-free rate + β × ERP

How big is the ERP? Three estimation routes, in rising order of Damodaran's preference:

  • Historical — average past stock returns minus bonds. Backward-looking, wildly sensitive to period chosen.
  • Survey — ask people. Anchored on yesterday's markets.
  • Implied — invert the DCF: today's index price plus expected cash flows tells you what premium the market is using. Damodaran recomputes this monthly; in recent years the US implied ERP has hovered around 4–5%.

Country risk: for a company whose cash flows are in a risky country, add the country default spread × equity multiplier (≈ 1.2) or use a country risk premium — his datasets publish these annually.

Keystone The anchors are estimates, not constants. Risk-free rate = government bond matched to currency and horizon, cleansed of default risk. ERP = the market's current price of average equity risk, best read implied from prices. Both move; a valuation built on stale anchors misprices everything.
Anti-pattern The 4% dogma. Plugging the historical US premium (or an Ibbotson number from a textbook) into every valuation regardless of what markets imply today. When implied ERP is 4.5% and you use 6%, every stock looks cheap — a bias wearing a suit of rigor.

Retrieve it

Why can't a corporate bond be the risk-free rate?

It carries default risk.

Its yield embeds a spread compensating for possible default — that's risk compensation, not the pure price of time. Mixing it into your "risk-free" anchor double-counts risk you'll add back via beta or default spreads.

What does an implied ERP of 5% mean, mechanically?

Markets pay for average risk at 5%.

Given index price, expected dividends/buybacks and growth, 5% is the premium that makes today's price equal the DCF of those cash flows. It's the market's own discount rate, revealed rather than assumed.

Valuing an Indian company in dollars — what must change?

Currency and country risk.

Discount rate and cash flows must share a currency (dollar risk-free rate, expected exchange-rate drift in cash flows), and India's country risk premium must enter the cost of equity unless the company's revenues are globally diversified.
